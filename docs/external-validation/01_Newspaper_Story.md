# Who Watches the Watchmen? A Diaspora Democracy Problem

*An accessible account of a constitutional design question emerging from a global Nepali organization's effort to hold secure online elections.*

---

Hundreds of thousands of Nepali citizens live outside Nepal — in Australia, Europe, North America, the Gulf states, Japan, and across the world. Many of them want to participate in organized community life, to elect representatives who speak for their interests, and to have a formal democratic voice in the affairs of their global diaspora.

One of the largest such organizations is designing a system to do exactly this: conduct secure, verifiable, anonymous elections entirely online, so that a Nepali living in Frankfurt can cast a meaningful vote alongside a Nepali in Toronto.

Building such a system is genuinely difficult. Online elections face real risks — of manipulation, of coercion, of vote-buying, of technical compromise. The organization has thought carefully about these risks and designed what appears to be a sophisticated constitutional framework for how elections will be governed.

But a constitutional design question has emerged that does not have an obvious answer.

---

## The Problem

In most democratic election systems, there are several distinct roles:

- Someone who **sets the rules** for what a valid election looks like
- Someone who **runs** the election according to those rules
- Someone who **certifies** that the election was conducted properly
- Someone who **investigates complaints** if voters or candidates believe something went wrong

Many constitutional systems assign these roles to separate bodies. The reasoning is that the people who investigate complaints can do so more credibly when they are not the same people whose conduct is being investigated, and that the bodies who certify elections operate more reliably when they are not controlled by the bodies who run them. Whether that separation is constitutionally required — or whether adequate procedural protections can substitute for structural separation — is itself a contested question in constitutional design.

In this organization's design, a single governing body — the Membership Assembly, which is the organization's highest sovereign authority — has accumulated responsibility for all of these roles, directly or indirectly. The Membership Assembly:

- Establishes the constitutional rules for elections
- Appoints the bodies that certify elections are valid
- Appoints the body responsible for interpreting constitutional disputes
- Controls the amendment process that could change any of these arrangements

None of this happened through negligence or bad faith. It happened because the Membership Assembly is the organization's legitimate constitutional authority. Of course it controls these things. Who else would?

---

## The Deeper Question

Here is where the constitutional design question becomes interesting.

The organization has created a Constitutional Interpretation Committee — a body that interprets what the constitution means when disputes arise. This committee is supposed to be independent. It is supposed to be able to rule against the Membership Assembly if the Assembly acts unconstitutionally.

But the Constitutional Interpretation Committee is appointed by the Membership Assembly. And the committee interprets the constitutional provisions that describe how independent the committee is supposed to be.

In other words: the organization that could benefit from limiting the committee's independence also appoints the committee, and the committee determines for itself how independent it is required to be.

This is not unique to this organization. It is a version of a problem that appears in constitutional design everywhere. In most established democracies, it is handled through long historical processes, independent legal traditions, professional judicial cultures, and the accumulated weight of institutional history.

This organization has none of those things yet. It is a new constitutional design, built from scratch.

---

## The Unresolved Question

Constitutional architects studying this design have identified two broad approaches to the problem:

**Approach A:** Require that the bodies responsible for election oversight have genuinely independent constitutional sources — authority that does not trace through the Membership Assembly. This is structurally cleaner but requires creating new governing bodies, each of which raises its own legitimacy questions.

**Approach B:** Keep the single constitutional authority structure but add strong constitutional protections — near-unanimity requirements for changes, explicit independence mandates, appointment procedures designed to limit ongoing control. Accept that independence here means operational independence within a shared constitutional framework, not independence of origin.

Both approaches are used in real constitutional systems around the world. Neither is obviously wrong. The question is which is right for this specific organization, at this specific stage of its constitutional development.

The architects of this system have concluded that they cannot answer this question through technical analysis alone. It is a constitutional design judgment — a question about what kind of trust anchor a democratic organization is allowed to rest on.

---

## Why This Matters

This is not an abstract governance puzzle. Millions of diaspora citizens around the world participate in similar organizations. The question of how to design genuinely independent election oversight for organizations that operate across borders, without the backing of state authority, without established legal traditions, and often with limited resources — is one that democratic governance practitioners will increasingly face.

If this organization gets the design right, it may produce a model that other diaspora communities can learn from. If it gets it wrong, the risk is not catastrophic failure — it is something more subtle: an election system that appears legitimate and functions correctly under normal conditions but is structurally vulnerable to slow drift over time. Members continue voting, seeing certified results and completed appeals, unaware that the institutional safeguards they originally relied on may no longer operate in quite the same way.

The challenge is particularly acute for diaspora organizations. A national democratic system typically has courts, a free press, civil society organizations, opposition parties, and public scrutiny operating as external checks. A diaspora organization often has none of these — only volunteers, rotating leadership, limited institutional memory, and members scattered across dozens of countries who have no easy way to observe long-term constitutional drift. What looks like a manageable risk inside a mature nation-state may carry different constitutional weight for a young organization with no equivalent corrective mechanisms.

That is the problem worth paying attention to.
