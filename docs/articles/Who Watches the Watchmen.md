# Who Watches the Watchmen?

## A Diaspora Democracy Problem

**By Dr. Nab Raj Roshyara**

---

*A simple explanation of a hard question that appeared while building secure online elections for a worldwide Nepali organization.*

"Who watches the watchmen?" is a very old question. In any system, some people are given the job of watching over others. Their work is to check that the rules are followed. But then a harder question appears: **who watches those people?** Who makes sure that the watchers themselves follow the rules?

This article is about that question. It is about *auditing* — the work of checking, carefully and honestly, whether an election was run correctly. And it is about a problem that has no easy answer.

---

## A Short Word About Me

 I am a software architect — a person who designs the structure of large computer systems. For some time, I have been building an online election system for a worldwide Nepali community. My special focus is **auditing**. I want every election to be something people can *check* and *trust*, not something they must simply *believe*.

While building this system, I met the question in this article. It surprised me. It is not a problem I can fix with code. It is a deeper problem about trust and power. This article explains it in simple words, so that anyone can understand it — even a reader for whom English is a second language.

---

## Who This Is About

Hundreds of thousands of Nepali people live outside Nepal. They live in Australia, in Europe, in North America, in the Gulf countries, in Japan, and in many other places. Together they are called the *diaspora* — people who come from one country but now live far away, all around the world.

Many of them still want to take part in community life. They want to choose leaders who speak for them. They want a real, fair voice in the affairs of their global community.

One of the largest of these communities is now building a system to make this possible. The goal is to run elections completely online. The elections must be **secure** (safe from attack), **verifiable** (you can check that the result is true), and **anonymous** (no one can find out how a single person voted).

The dream is simple. A Nepali person living in Frankfurt should be able to vote, and that vote should count just as much as the vote of a Nepali person living in Toronto.

But building such a system is genuinely hard. Online elections face real dangers:

- **Manipulation** — someone secretly changing the result
- **Coercion** — someone forcing another person to vote in a certain way
- **Vote-buying** — someone paying people for their votes
- **Technical attack** — someone breaking into the computer system

The organization has to think about these dangers with great care. It should design a careful set of rules for how its elections will be governed.

And yet, one question has appeared that does not have an obvious answer.

---

## The Problem

In most election systems, there are several different jobs. Usually, different people or groups do each job:

- One group **sets the rules** — it decides what a fair election looks like
- One group **runs** the election — it carries out the vote, following those rules
- One group **certifies** the election — it officially confirms that everything was done correctly
- One group **handles complaints** — if a voter or a candidate believes something went wrong, this group investigates

Many countries give these four jobs to **separate** groups. The reason is simple and important.

The people who investigate complaints can be trusted more when they are *not* the same people they are investigating. And the group that confirms an election is honest works better when it is *not* controlled by the group that ran the election. In short: it is hard to check your own work fairly.

(Some experts argue that this separation must always exist. Others argue that strong rules and careful procedures can do the same job, even without separate groups. This itself is an open debate.)

Now look at the organization in this story.

Here, almost all of these jobs have ended up in the hands of **one single body**. It is called the **Membership Assembly**. It is the highest authority in the organization — the final source of all power. Step by step, directly or through groups it controls, the Membership Assembly has gathered nearly every job:

- It **writes the rules** for elections
- It **chooses the group** that confirms an election is valid
- It **chooses the group** that settles arguments about the rules
- It **controls how the rules can be changed**

This did not happen because anyone acted in bad faith. It happened for a very normal reason: the Membership Assembly is the legitimate, top authority of the organization. Of course it controls these things. Who else would?

---

## The Deeper Question

This is where the problem becomes interesting.

Suppose that the organization has created a special group called the **Constitutional Interpretation Committee**. ("Constitution" means the basic set of rules that the whole organization agrees to follow. To "interpret" it means to explain what those rules really mean when people disagree.)

This committee is meant to be **independent**. It is supposed to be able to say "no" to the Membership Assembly — even to rule *against* the Assembly if the Assembly breaks the rules.

But here is the difficulty.

The Membership Assembly **chooses the members** of this committee. And the committee is the body that decides **how independent it is allowed to be**.

So think about what this means. The body that might want less oversight is the same body that picks the watchers. And the watchers decide for themselves how much freedom they have. The watched are choosing the watchmen — and the watchmen are setting their own limits.

This problem is not unique to this organization. It appears, in some form, in almost every system of government in the world. In old and established democracies, it is kept under control by powerful forces: many years of history, strong and independent courts, a long tradition of honest judges, and the weight of trusted institutions built over generations.

This organization has none of those things yet. It is brand new. Its rules were written from the beginning, on an empty page.

---

## The Question With No Clear Answer

Experts who study this kind of system have found two main ways to solve the problem.

**Approach A — separate sources of power.** Give the groups that oversee elections their *own* independent source of authority. Their power should not flow down from the Membership Assembly. This is cleaner and safer in structure. But it means creating new governing bodies. And each new body brings its own hard question: where does *its* authority come from, and why should people trust it?

**Approach B — one source, but strong protections.** Keep the single top authority, but add strong locks around it. For example: require almost everyone to agree before any of these rules can be changed. Write clear, firm promises of independence. Design the selection process so the Assembly cannot keep controlling the watchers afterwards. In this approach, "independent" means independent in *daily work* — not independent in *origin*.

Both approaches are used in real countries today. Neither one is clearly wrong. The real question is narrower: **which approach is right for this organization, at this early stage of its life?**

The people designing this system have reached an honest conclusion. They cannot answer this question with technology or engineering alone. It is a question of values and judgment. In the end, it is a question about trust: *where is a democratic organization allowed to place the final trust that holds everything together?*

---

## Why This Matters

This is not just a puzzle for experts. Millions of diaspora people around the world belong to organizations like this one.

These organizations face a shared challenge. They work across many countries. They have no government standing behind them. They have no long legal tradition. They often have little money. And still, they must find a way to build election oversight that people can truly trust. More and more communities will face exactly this challenge in the coming years.

If this organization solves the problem well, it may become a model — an example that other diaspora communities can study and follow.

If it solves the problem badly, the danger is not a sudden, dramatic collapse. The danger is quieter, and harder to see. The election system would still *look* correct. It would still *work* on a normal day. Results would still be confirmed. Complaints would still be closed. But slowly, over many years, the protections that people first relied on could weaken — and most members would never notice. They would keep voting, trusting a safety net that is no longer as strong as they believe.

The problem is especially serious for diaspora organizations. A normal country has many outside guards working at the same time: independent courts, a free press, citizen groups, opposition parties, and the watchful eyes of the public. A diaspora organization usually has none of these. It has only volunteers, leaders who change often, a short memory of its own past, and members spread across dozens of countries — people who have no simple way to watch for slow change over time.

So a risk that seems small and manageable inside a strong, mature nation may be far more dangerous for a young organization that has no such guards to catch its mistakes.

That is the problem worth paying attention to.
