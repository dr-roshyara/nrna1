# The Question That Changed My Understanding of Politics

## How trying to build a political score led me to discover I was measuring the wrong thing

**By Dr. Nab Raj Roshyara**

---

Growing up in Nepal, like many people of my generation, I saw political debates everywhere. In teashops, in living rooms, on long bus rides — everyone had opinions about leaders. Everyone could say who was honest and who was corrupt, who was capable and who was hopeless. Yet one thing always surprised me: almost nobody could explain *how* those judgments were formed. We all judged politicians constantly. None of us could say what our judgments were actually made of.

That curiosity stayed with me for years — long before it became a research project.

Perhaps the obvious path would have been to study political science. Instead, I chose mathematics. Many people thought the two had nothing to do with each other. I suspected that one day they might.

Years later — by then designing large software systems for a living — I finally decided to take the question seriously, the only way I knew how. I wanted to turn it into something precise: **What makes a good political leader — and can it be measured?**

---

## The Confident Beginning

At first, the answer appeared obvious.

A good leader should be honest. Competent. Empathetic. Resilient. Patient. Able to communicate, to make decisions, to lead.

Like many researchers before me, I believed political leadership could be represented as a collection of desirable human characteristics. The intuition was attractive because it reflects how people actually talk. Ask anyone to describe a good leader, and they will name personality traits — not institutional duties.

So my first attempt was built exactly that way. Rate each leader on a list of traits. Weight the ratings. Combine them into a single number — a political score.

It seemed reasonable. It seemed scientific. And it was wrong in ways that took me years to understand.

---

## The First Crack

As the framework grew, an uncomfortable question emerged: are these characteristics really independent of each other?

The answer was no.

Competence influences decision-making. Decision-making influences execution. Execution influences outcomes. Communication influences trust. Empathy influences communication. Resilience influences decision-making. Almost every characteristic influenced every other characteristic.

The framework had become a dense web of interdependent concepts. And that is a serious problem, because when dimensions overlap, a weighted average quietly counts the same phenomenon several times. The final score looks precise — but the precision is an illusion, inflated by hidden double-counting.

I had brought mathematics into the project expecting it to be my calculator.

Instead, it became my critic.

**The mathematics did not prove my model. It destroyed it.**

---

## The Student's Question

Around that time, I explained the scoring model to a political science student. She listened patiently, and then asked a question I have never forgotten.

"Suppose a leader receives a score of 82," she said. "What *is* the 82? Is it morality? Competence? Popularity? Leadership? Public satisfaction?"

I started to answer — and stopped. I had no answer.

The score summarized information without explaining what it fundamentally measured. And a number without a clearly defined object of measurement has very little scientific meaning.

Her question followed me around for months. It slowly turned into a harder version of itself, the version mathematics had been whispering all along: **what exactly are you measuring?**

---

## The Day I Realized I Was Measuring the Wrong Thing

The search for an answer gradually pulled me away from personality psychology, for one simple reason.

Political leadership is not a human personality. It is a **public function**.

A prime minister, a judge, a police commissioner, and a mayor perform fundamentally different responsibilities. Yet my framework was evaluating all of them with the same psychological traits — as if governing a country and administering justice were the same job done by different personalities.

That was the deepest flaw of all. The evaluation should not begin with the individual.

It should begin with the **office**.

---

## The Turn

This led to a profound change of perspective. Instead of asking *"What characteristics define a good leader?"* I began asking:

**What responsibilities define a public office?**

Suddenly, everything became simpler.

Every public office exists because society delegates specific responsibilities to it. A minister is expected to govern. A judge is expected to administer justice. A police officer is expected to protect public safety. A mayor is expected to coordinate local administration.

Responsibilities — not personality — define the purpose of public office.

---

## Responsibility Before Evaluation

From this shift, a principle emerged that now sits at the center of my work:

**Evaluation cannot exist without responsibility.** A public servant can only be evaluated fairly if the responsibilities of the office are first understood.

Every office has its own space of responsibilities. A minister's is not a judge's. A mayor's is not a police commissioner's. I began calling this the office's **Responsibility Space** — the collection of constitutional, legal, institutional, ethical, and operational responsibilities that belong to that office and to no other.

Once the responsibilities of an office are written down, political performance stops being a matter of impressions. It becomes measurable: the degree to which those responsibilities are fulfilled.

---

## From Scores to Gaps

Once responsibilities were defined, one more realization followed naturally.

Instead of asking *"How good is this leader?"* I could ask: **"How completely have the responsibilities of this office been fulfilled?"**

For every responsibility there exists:

- an expected level of fulfillment,
- an observed level of fulfillment,
- and the difference between them.

This difference — the **Responsibility Gap** — turned out to be more informative than any score. A score merely describes the current state. A gap identifies the distance to be improved, and points at exactly *which* duty is falling short.

If that student asked me her question today, I would finally have an answer. An 82, on its own, means nothing. But *"this office has fulfilled this specific responsibility to this degree, and here is the gap that remains"* — that means something. And it changes the goal of the whole exercise: from judgment to improvement.

---

## An Idea From an Unexpected Place

One more thing shaped this journey, and it came from an unexpected direction.

While working as a software architect, I had absorbed an idea that unexpectedly changed how I thought about politics: **before building a system, you must first understand the domain itself.** What things exist in it? What is each one responsible for? Which concepts belong together — and which must be kept apart?

Applied to politics, this discipline forced me to stop mixing everything into one tangled model. Personality, institutions, responsibilities, evidence, performance, and scoring were separated into distinct layers, each with its own purpose, connected to the others through explicit relationships.

It sounds simple. It is simple. But that separation — obvious to a software architect, rare in political measurement — is what finally made the framework coherent.

---

## What This Became

My first attempt at a trait-based score eventually became the earliest version of what is now called **PolitPass** — not a legal qualification for public office, and not another popularity rating, but a civic framework for helping citizens understand how responsibly the public offices around them are being exercised.

PolitPass gradually became something much bigger than a scoring system. The score is now the *last* step of the process, not the first. The foundation lies much deeper — in understanding what public responsibility means, how it can be observed, how fulfillment can be measured, and how a society can continuously learn from the gap between expectation and reality.

Along the way, my view of political leaders themselves changed. I once saw them as individuals possessing desirable traits. Today I see them as **temporary stewards** of public responsibilities — people who operate, for a limited time, within institutions created by citizens. Their performance is determined not by charisma or popularity, but by how effectively they fulfill the responsibilities entrusted to them.

The object of evaluation is no longer the person. It is the fulfillment of public responsibility.

---

## The Wrong Question

Looking back, I now realize that I spent years trying to answer the wrong question.

I wanted to know how to measure politicians.

Mathematics kept insisting on a different question: **what exactly are you measuring?**

That simple question changed everything.

I started this journey believing that better politics required better politicians.

Today I think better politics begins with something simpler: understanding public responsibility.

Everything else follows from there.

---

**Dr. Nab Raj Roshyara** is a software architect and researcher working on formal models of public responsibility and on the constitutional governance design of secure, anonymous online elections for cross-border democratic organizations.
