Given everything you've been working on over the past year, I would actually recommend **not lobbying for a particular election system**.

Instead, write as a journalist who advocates for **trustworthy governance**.

There is an important difference.

* **Lobbyist:** "Our system is the best."
* **Journalist:** "Here are the characteristics a trustworthy election system should have."

The second approach is more credible, more ethical, and will likely have greater long-term influence.

Since your research has focused on constitutional governance, DDD, auditability, transparency, and diaspora elections, you have a unique opportunity to educate readers without appearing promotional.

Here are the article series I would write.

---

# Series 1 — Why Trust Matters More Than Technology

Examples:

* **An election is only as strong as the trust people place in it.**
* **Why transparency alone does not guarantee trust.**
* **Can a perfectly secure election still lose legitimacy?**

Purpose:

Explain that legitimacy is a social and constitutional concept, not merely a technical one.

---

# Series 2 — Lessons from Around the World

Examples:

* How different countries organize election oversight.
* Different models of electoral commissions.
* Why appeals and dispute resolution matter.
* What diaspora elections can learn from public elections.

Purpose:

Present comparative analysis rather than prescriptions.

---

# Series 3 — Understanding Election Disputes

This aligns well with your work on Contestation and Adjudication.

Examples:

* What happens when someone challenges an election result?
* Why every election needs an independent dispute process.
* What makes an appeal process fair?

Most readers have never thought about these questions.

---

# Series 4 — Invisible Parts of an Election

People see voting.

They rarely see:

* evidence preservation
* audit trails
* chain of custody
* recount procedures
* correction mechanisms
* accountability

These topics make excellent educational articles.

---

# Series 5 — Myth vs Reality

Examples:

* "Electronic voting is always insecure."
* "Paper ballots automatically guarantee trust."
* "Transparency solves everything."
* "Technology alone can eliminate election fraud."

These can be balanced, evidence-based pieces.

---

# Series 6 — Governance Before Software

This is where your own experience is valuable.

Possible title:

> **Before Writing a Single Line of Code, We Spent Hundreds of Hours Designing Governance**

Explain that the hardest problems were not programming—they were defining responsibilities, accountability, appeals, and constitutional safeguards.

This can help readers appreciate why governance should precede implementation.

---

# Series 7 — Building Trust Incrementally

Possible articles:

* Why independent audits matter.
* Why openness matters.
* Why documenting decisions matters.
* Why clear responsibilities matter.
* Why admitting uncertainty builds credibility.

These reinforce trust without advocating for a specific product.

---

# Topics to avoid

If your goal is to increase trust, I would avoid articles like:

* "Our architecture is revolutionary."
* "Our platform is the most trustworthy."
* "Everyone should adopt our solution."

Those sound promotional and can reduce credibility.

Instead, write:

> "These are the principles any trustworthy election system should satisfy."

Then let readers evaluate systems against those principles.

---

# A flagship article I would recommend

Given your background, I think one article could become foundational:

## **What Makes an Election Worth Trusting?**

A possible structure:

1. Trust is earned, not declared.
2. Technology is necessary but insufficient.
3. Every election should answer five questions:

   * Can decisions be independently reviewed?
   * Can evidence be preserved?
   * Are responsibilities clearly separated?
   * Can mistakes be corrected transparently?
   * Can the public understand the process?
4. Trust is built through governance, transparency, accountability, and continuous improvement.
5. These principles apply whether elections use paper, electronic systems, or hybrid approaches.

This educates readers while avoiding advocacy for any single implementation.

---

## My recommendation

If your long-term goal is to influence the NRNA and similar organizations, position yourself as someone who explains **principles of trustworthy elections**, not as someone campaigning for a particular technical solution. If your ideas are sound, readers and decision-makers will naturally begin to ask how existing systems measure up against those principles. That approach is typically more persuasive and more sustainable than direct lobbying.
