## Rationale
The book is organized into four parts: Part I Perspective (ch.1 what is interesting about mathematics for a philosopher; ch.2 a potpourri of central questions -- necessity/a priori knowledge, objects/objectivity/truth, the mathematical vs the physical, theorems/theories/concepts); Part II History (ch.3 Plato and Aristotle; ch.4 Kant and Mill); Part III The Big Three (ch.5 Logicism -- Frege, Russell, Carnap/logical positivism; ch.6 Formalism -- Hilbert's Grundlagen der Geometrie, finitism, incompleteness, Curry; ch.7 Intuitionism -- Brouwer, Heyting, Dummett); Part IV The Contemporary Scene (ch.8 realism about mathematical objects -- Godel, web of belief, set-theoretic realism; ch.9 anti-realism -- fictionalism, modal construction; ch.10 Structuralism -- ante rem structures, structuralism without structures, knowledge of structures via pattern recognition and implicit definition), explicitly identified by the author as his own position ('I have saved the best for last'). [S0705] Frames Chapter 1's opening problem: rationalism attempts to extend mathematics' perceived methodology (proof, pure reason) to all of knowledge (Descartes, Spinoza, Leibniz), while empiricism holds sense experience, not pure reason, is the source of knowledge (Locke, Berkeley, Hume, Mill, later logical positivists); mathematics is an apparent counterexample to empiricism since mathematical knowledge seems based on proof rather than observation, making it a paradigm case of a priori knowledge (knowledge prior to and independent of experience) that empiricists have historically struggled to accommodate. [S0705] Gives three reasons for the mathematics-philosophy connection: (1) both fields share ancient shared origins and undergo definitional transformations over what counts as each discipline; (2) mathematics is a clean case study for general philosophical questions of reference (what does a term stand for/represent?) and normativity (what obligates correct reasoning?), since mathematics and logic provide simple, well-formulated cases; (3) mathematics is epistemologically central because it is a primary tool for scientific understanding of the physical, human, and social world -- quoting Galileo's 'the book of nature is written in the language of mathematics' as an insightful but unsolved metaphor for why mathematics plays this role.
