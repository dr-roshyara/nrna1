Note: Extracts an implicit (non-Shannon) theory of information from the same Roopa Pai Vedas/Upanishads source's treatment of oral transmission, distinct from that file's knowledge-hierarchy extraction. Central claim: information is a representation transmissible between knowers but not identical with knowledge. Models the Vedic sound-based information carrier as multi-dimensional (words+pronunciation+rhythm+tone+emphasis+sequence, with Sama Veda melody notation as an encoding example), subject to transmission degradation (loss/distortion/addition/reinterpretation), for which the tradition's redundant multi-pattern recitation functioned as an error-detection/integrity mechanism (Original->multiple recitation Patterns A/B/C->cross-check->integrity). Enumerates five information channels (oral, musical, written, demonstrative, dialogical) noting the Upanishadic teacher-student form is INTERACTIVE (question-driven), not one-way broadcast. States the central distinction 'successful information transmission != successful knowledge transmission', splitting Information Transmission (Sender->Signal->Receiver) from Knowledge Transmission (Teacher->Signal->Student->Interpretation->Understanding->Experience->Realization). Adds a syntax/semantics/pragmatics decomposition of a signal, and states information is inherently contextual (Message+Context+Knower=Interpretation). Uses the historical example of ritual practice persisting after its meaning was lost (~700 BCE) as a concrete case of 'procedure preserved != purpose understood' (artifact integrity != semantic integrity), motivating a three-part integrity requirement: representation integrity (was what was said preserved), semantic integrity (was what it meant preserved), and intent integrity (was why it was said preserved). States information quality depends on the receiver, not just the message (World->Senses->Perception->Mind->Interpretation->Understanding, each stage introducing possible distortion), and that information is broader than language (linguistic/visual/auditory/behavioral/emotional/contextual/experiential), reformulated generally as 'information is a detectable difference that can alter the state of a receiver.' Concludes with a formal extracted definition ('information is a structured representation...carrying distinctions, meaning, or experience from a source to a receiver through a transmission channel; information becomes knowledge only when the receiver successfully integrates it into understanding') and a three-layer architecture (Information: representation/encoding/signal/storage/transmission/integrity -> Knowledge: meaning/context/understanding/validation/experience/reasoning -> Wisdom: judgment/action/transformation/capability/realization), plus four preserved principles: preserving information != preserving knowledge; preserving representation != preserving meaning; transmitting meaning != producing understanding; knowledge exists only when information has transformed the knower's capability.
