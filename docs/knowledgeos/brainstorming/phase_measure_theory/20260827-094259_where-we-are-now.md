# 1. The deepest problem: what exactly is the object being assigned probability?

This is now the most important mathematical question.

Suppose:

> "Nexus is running version 3.69."

What is:

$$
P=0.85?
$$

Is it:

### A. Probability that the proposition is true?

$$
P(p\mid E)=0.85
$$

### B. Confidence in the determination?

$$
Conf(F)=0.85
$$

### C. Strength of the evidence?

$$
Strength(E,F)=0.85
$$

### D. Degree of truth?

$$
T(p)=0.85
$$

These are **four different things**.

They may all use \([0,1]\), but they are not mathematically interchangeable.

So we cannot yet say:

> "Facts have probability."

We can say:

> **A fact/proposition may have a probabilistic epistemic assessment under a probabilistic regime.**

That is much safer.

---

# 2. The second problem: what is the logical object?

You said:

> "First you estimate the logic behind the reason of the facts."

This is very important.

Suppose:

$$
F_1=A
$$

and:

$$
R_1:A\rightarrow B.
$$

Then:

$$
F_1,R_1\vdash B.
$$

That's deterministic logical reasoning.

But suppose \(A\) itself has uncertain status:

$$
P(A)=0.8.
$$

Then the logic still says:

$$
A\rightarrow B.
$$

But **logic alone does not tell us**:

$$
P(B)=0.8.
$$

We need a probabilistic model.

So we have two distinct structures:

$$
\boxed{
\text{Logical entailment}
}
$$

and

$$
\boxed{
\text{Uncertainty propagation}.
}
$$

This is a critical separation.

# 3. The third problem: "reason" is still underspecified

You have now defined reason as logic.

Good.

But we must distinguish:

$$
\text{Logic}
$$

from:

$$
\text{Premises}.
$$

Logic tells us **how premises imply conclusions**.

For example:

$$
A,\ A\rightarrow B\vdash B.
$$

But where do \(A\) and \(A\rightarrow B\) come from?

They may come from:

* observations;
* previously determined facts;
* documented rules;
* principles;
* laws;
* methods;
* institutional decisions.

So perhaps:

$$
\boxed{
Reasoning = Premises + Inference Rules
}
$$

rather than:

$$
Reason = Logic.
$$

This distinction will become important when we formalize KnowledgeOS.

We need to discover **what the value means**.

# 5. The fifth problem: the ideal state is still undefined

You introduced:

$$
K_t^*

as the ideal Knowledge state.

This is a very strong idea.

But now we need to ask:

> If \(K_t^*\) is ideal, does every proposition in it have value 0 or 1?

Possibly.

But what about questions whose answer is fundamentally uncertain?

For example:

> "Will event X happen tomorrow?"

Even an ideal Knowledge state at time \(t\) may not contain a deterministic answer if the future is genuinely indeterminate.

So perhaps:

$$
K_t^*
$$

does **not** mean:

$$
\forall p,\quad V(p)\in\{0,1\}.
$$

Instead it may mean:

$$
\boxed{

$$

That may be a much stronger definition.

Then:

$$
V_t(p)=0.8
$$

could actually be the **correct ideal state at time \(t\)**, rather than an imperfect approximation.

This is a crucial issue.

# 9. The seventh problem: determination is recursive

This remains perhaps the most architecturally important discovery.

Suppose:

$$
F_1
$$

was determined from observations.

Then:

$$
F_2
$$

is determined using \(F_1\).

Then:

$$
F_3
$$

uses \(F_2\).

So:

$$
\boxed{
O
\rightarrow F_1
\rightarrow F_2
\rightarrow F_3
\rightarrow\cdots
}
$$

This creates a **determination history**.

And if:

$$
F_3
$$

is later challenged, we need to reconstruct:

$$
F_3
\leftarrow F_2
\leftarrow F_1
\leftarrow O.
$$

This is where provenance, temporal identity, revision, and reconstruction suddenly become mathematically necessary rather than merely architectural preferences.
