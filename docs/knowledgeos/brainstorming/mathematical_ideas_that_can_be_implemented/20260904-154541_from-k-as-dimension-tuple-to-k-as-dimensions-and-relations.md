Yes. I think this is an important refinement of the model.

If we speak about **influencing factors**, then a dimension does not have meaning in isolation. Its epistemic significance arises through **relations**—with another factor, a question, a reference, a context, or a comparison.

So we should move from:

$$
K=(d_1,d_2,\ldots,d_n)
$$

toward something like:

$$
\boxed{K=(D,R)}
$$

where:

* \(D\) = dimensions/factors
* \(R\) = relations among those factors.

### The key idea

A factor by itself is not yet knowledge.

For example:

$$
d_1=\text{evidence}
$$

does not tell us much until we have a relation such as:

$$
R(d_1,d_2)
$$

where \(d_2\) might be a claim.

Or:

$$
R(d_1,d_2\mid Q)
$$

might tell us how evidence relates to the question.

Thus:

$$
\boxed{\text{Knowledge emerges from distinguishable factors + relations between them.}}
$$

This is very close to the direction you are developing.

---

### Then what does Zero mean?

If we set:

$$
d_3=0
$$

we are saying that **the influence of \(d_3\) is neutralized relative to the current relational configuration**.

But this is crucial:

$$
d_3=0
$$

doesn't mean that \(d_3\) has no existence or meaning.

It means, for the current inquiry:

$$
R(d_3,d_j)\approx 0
$$

for the relevant relationships.

And when we activate another dimension:

$$
d_3\neq0
$$

the relationship structure can change.

---

### Why “keeping the other dimensions zero” becomes interesting

Suppose:

$$
K=(d_1,d_2,d_3,d_4).
$$

We focus on \(d_1,d_2\):

$$
K_{12}=(d_1,d_2,0,0).
$$

Now we can study:

$$
R(d_1,d_2).
$$

Then activate \(d_3\):

$$
K_{123}=(d_1,d_2,d_3,0)
$$

and ask whether:

$$
R(d_1,d_2)
$$

changes because of \(d_3\).

This is exactly where **interaction** becomes observable.

$$
\boxed{
\text{Zeroing dimensions can expose relationships among the remaining dimensions.}
}
$$

And then we can test whether the relationship itself produces something that neither isolated factor provides.

---

## Your stronger statement: “without relationship, you have no knowledge”

I would make it slightly more precise:

$$
\boxed{
\text{A distinguishable factor becomes epistemically informative only through a relation to something else under a question or purpose.}
}
$$

Because there is one subtle exception: a single factor can contain an internally structured relation—for example, a temporal sequence or an internally differentiated observation. So “two factors” should not become a rigid axiom.

The deeper principle is:

$$
\boxed{\text{No distinction without relation.}}
$$

And therefore:

$$
\boxed{\text{No epistemically meaningful distinction without a relation that makes the distinction relevant.}}
$$

---

### This changes our Knowledge Algebra substantially

Instead of starting with an algebra of objects:

$$
\mathfrak{KA}=(K,\oplus,\ldots)
$$

we may need to investigate a **relational Knowledge Algebra**:

$$
\boxed{
\mathfrak{KA}
=
(\mathcal D,\mathcal R,\mathcal Q,\mathcal T,\mathcal O,\mathcal I)
}
$$

where:

* \(\mathcal D\): dimensions/factors
* \(\mathcal R\): relations
* \(\mathcal Q\): questions/reference frames
* \(\mathcal T\): transformations
* \(\mathcal O\): observations
* \(\mathcal I\): invariants.

Then:

$$
\text{Knowledge}
\sim
\text{Factors}+\text{Relations}+\text{Question/Frame}.
$$

Not necessarily literally an addition—we should not assume the algebra yet.

---

## And this gives us a deeper interpretation of Focus

**Focus is not merely looking at fewer dimensions.**

It is:

$$
\boxed{
Focus_S(K)
=
\text{activate a selected relational subspace while neutralizing other influences}.
}
$$

Then:

$$
Focus_{\{d_1,d_2\}}
$$

asks:

> What knowledge can emerge from the relationship between \(d_1\) and \(d_2\) when the other influences are held neutral?

And **surface view** becomes:

$$
Surface(K)=\text{observe the full available relational configuration}.
$$

So we now have a very interesting hierarchy:

$$
\boxed{
Factor
\rightarrow
Relation
\rightarrow
Interaction
\rightarrow
Determination
\rightarrow
Knowledge
}
$$

while:

$$
\boxed{
Zero
\rightarrow
neutralize\ influence
\rightarrow
isolate\ relation
\rightarrow
Focus
\rightarrow
observe\ what\ emerges.
}
$$

That is, I think, a stronger foundation for Axis C than treating the dimensions as merely independent coordinates. The **relations may actually be the primary object of investigation**, while the dimensions are the participants in those relations.
