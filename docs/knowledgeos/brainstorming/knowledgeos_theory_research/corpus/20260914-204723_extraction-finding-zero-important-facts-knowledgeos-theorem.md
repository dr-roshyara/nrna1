## Part 1: The Discovery — K-127 as an Empirical Artifact

### 1.1 The Artifact Itself

| Property | Details |
|:---|:---|
| **Name** | K-127 (Cœdès catalog number) |
| **Location** | Trapang Prei, Sambor on Mekong, Cambodia |
| **Date** | 683 CE (çaka era 605 + 78) |
| **Language** | Old Khmer (not Sanskrit) |
| **Content** | Land grant inscription with date |
| **Key Numeral** | 605 — the zero is a dot |
| **Discovery** | 1891 by Adhémar Leclère |
| **Publication** | 1931 by George Cœdès |
| **Rediscovery** | 2013 by Amir Aczel |

### 1.2 The Epistemic Significance

The artifact establishes:

1. **The zero existed in 683 CE** — two centuries before the Gwalior zero (876 CE)
2. **The zero existed independently of Arab trade** — predates Arab empire expansion
3. **The zero existed in an "Indianized" civilization** — but the inscription is in Old Khmer, not Sanskrit
4. **The zero was used as a place-holder** — in the number 605, the dot represents "no tens"

**KnowledgeOS Application:** This is a case study in **provenance verification**. The artifact's significance depends entirely on:
- Authenticity (is it real?)
- Dating (is it from 683 CE?)
- Interpretation (is the dot actually a zero?)
- Context (was it influenced by India or independent?)

## Part 2: The Debate — East vs. West Origin of the Zero

### 2.1 The Historical Positions

| Position | Proponents | Claim | Evidence |
|:---|:---|:---|:---|
| **Eastern Origin** | Moritz Cantor, Louis Karpinski, George Cœdès | Zero invented in India/SE Asia | K-127 (683 CE), Bakhshali manuscript |
| **Western/Arab Origin** | G. R. Kaye | Zero invented in Europe or Arabia | No earlier zero in India (Gwalior 876 CE) |
| **Independent Invention** | Modern scholars | Zero invented multiple times | Maya zero (37 BCE) |

### 2.2 G. R. Kaye's Argument (The Skeptic)

Kaye argued:
- No datable zero earlier than 876 CE in India
- Therefore, the zero must have come from Europe or Arabia
- India was a cultural backwater that received mathematical ideas from Greece
- The Bakhshali manuscript was no older than the 12th century

**Kaye's Methodological Error:** He inferred absence of evidence = evidence of absence. He also ignored the possibility that the zero existed in non-datable artifacts.

### 2.3 Cœdès's Refutation (The Discovery)

Cœdès found K-127 (683 CE) and K-128 (684 CE), which:
- Predated the Gwalior zero by two centuries
- Predated the Arab empire's expansion
- Were in Old Khmer, not Sanskrit — local invention
- Were published in 1931, defeating Kaye's hypothesis

**KnowledgeOS Application:** This is a classic case of **epistemic revision** — new evidence (K-127) overturned a previously accepted theory (Kaye's Western origin). The theory had been "closed" but was reopened by empirical evidence.

## Part 6: The Cœdès-Kaye Controversy — Epistemic Lessons

### 6.1 The Controversy Timeline

| Year | Event | Epistemic Significance |
|:---|:---|:---|
| 1891 | K-127 discovered by Leclère | Artifact exists |
| 1919 | Kaye publishes "Indian Mathematics" | Skeptical argument |
| 1931 | Cœdès publishes K-127 analysis | Refutation of Kaye |
| 1975-1979 | Khmer Rouge destroys artifacts | K-127 lost |
| 2013 | Aczel rediscovered K-127 | Verification |

### 6.2 Epistemic Lessons

1. **Absence of evidence ≠ evidence of absence.** Kaye's argument was that no zero earlier than 876 CE had been found. This was true at the time, but it was a temporary state of knowledge.

2. **Provenance matters.** K-127's significance depends on its provenance — where it was found, when it was dated, and how it was interpreted.

3. **Interpretation is not observation.** The dot in 605 is observed; the interpretation that it is a zero is an inference.

4. **The artifact was lost and rediscovered.** K-127 was lost during the Khmer Rouge period and rediscovered by Aczel in 2013. This is a case study in **epistemic retrieval**.

5. **Authority matters.** Cœdès's authority as a scholar lent weight to his interpretation. Kaye's authority as the first scholar to study the Bakhshali manuscript lent weight to his skepticism.

## Part 7: The Zero as a Place-Holder

### 7.1 The Place-Holder Function

> "The zero is not only a concept of nothingness, which allows us to do arithmetic efficiently, but is also a place-holding device that enables our base-10 number system to work."

**Example:**
- 5 = five
- 50 = five tens, zero units
- 505 = five hundreds, zero tens, five units

### 7.2 The Power of the Zero

> "The existence of a place-holding zero is what gives meaning even to numbers that contain no zeros in them. The number 143, for example, could not be written this way if the number 140 didn't exist, and this number needs the zero as a place-holder for the empty units."

**KnowledgeOS Application:** The zero functions as a **placeholder for absence**. In KnowledgeOS, this is exactly the function of `Unknown` — it is a placeholder for the absence of knowledge. The distinction is:
- `Absent` = there is nothing (no assertion)
- `Unknown` = there is a placeholder (an assertion with Σ = Unknown)

## Part 8: The Bakhshali Manuscript — An Undateable Artifact

### 8.1 The Problem

The Bakhshali manuscript:
- Written on birch bark
- Contains a zero symbol
- Cannot be carbon-dated because it is too fragile
- Scholars disagree on its date (200 BCE to 1200 CE)

### 8.2 The Epistemic Problem

The manuscript's significance depends entirely on its date. If it is from 200 BCE, it proves that the zero was invented in India early. If it is from 1200 CE, it proves nothing.

**KnowledgeOS Application:** This is a case study in **epistemic dependency** — the significance of a proposition depends on other propositions (e.g., the date of the manuscript). KnowledgeOS must represent these dependencies.
