# KnowledgeOS — Step 459

## Structural Equivalence, Graph Isomorphism, Homomorphism, Simulation, Bisimulation, Refinement, Compatibility, Structural Correspondence and Safe Structure Transformation

We continue the reduction programme from Step 458.

The previous step established that KnowledgeOS can represent many simultaneous structures through:

$$
\boxed{\mathfrak K_{\min}=(ID,\mathcal R^\star,\mathsf{Sem})}
$$

and that graphs are projections rather than ontology primitives.

Now we face a deeper problem:

> If two systems represent apparently the same domain structure in different ways, how can KnowledgeOS determine whether they are actually equivalent, merely similar, refinements of each other, compatible, or semantically different?

This is essential for:

* integrating two databases;
* migrating systems;
* schema evolution;
* ontology alignment;
* mergers;
* event replay;
* model transformation;
* ML-generated structures;
* distributed KnowledgeOS nodes;
* architecture decisions;
* avoiding semantic corruption.

The central attack is:

$$
\boxed{
\text{Can structural equivalence itself be reduced to}
ID+\mathcal R^\star+\mathsf{Sem}?
}
$$

---

# 459.1 First principle: "same structure" is ambiguous

Consider these two representations.

### Representation A

```text
Customer
   |
   +-- owns --> Account
```

### Representation B

```text
Account
   |
   +-- ownedBy --> Customer
```

Are they the same?

Possibly.

If:

$$
OwnedBy(x,y)\iff Owns(y,x)
$$

under the semantic contract, then they may be semantically equivalent.

But consider:

```text
Customer
   |
   +-- manages --> Account
```

The graph shape is identical.

The semantics are not.

Therefore:

$$
\boxed{
SameGraphShape\neq SameSemantics.
}
$$

This confirms Step 458.

---

# 459.2 Representation

### Definition

A **representation** is a concrete encoding of some semantic content or structure.

Examples:

* relational database;
* JSON document;
* RDF graph;
* event stream;
* object graph;
* knowledge graph;
* XML;
* mathematical structure.

Let:

$$
Rep(X)
$$

denote a representation of semantic object \(X\).

Two representations can differ syntactically while expressing the same semantic content.

Therefore:

$$
RepresentationEquality
\neq
SemanticEquality.
$$

---

# 459.3 Structural equivalence

### Definition

Two structures are **structurally equivalent** under a contract if their relevant structural relationships correspond in a way that preserves the properties declared relevant by that contract.

Symbolically:

$$
A\equiv_{\Gamma}^{struct}B.
$$

The critical word is **relevant**.

A database migration might care about:

* entity identity;
* mandatory relations;
* cardinality;
* temporal semantics.

It might not care about:

* physical table names;
* column order;
* indexes.

Therefore structural equivalence is contract-relative.

---

# 459.4 Semantic equivalence

We already have the broader notion:

$$
A\equiv_{\Gamma}^{sem}B.
$$

### Definition

Two representations are semantically equivalent under \(\Gamma\) when no distinction relevant to the declared semantic contract can distinguish them.

This is stronger than string equality and usually different from physical equality.

Example:

```text
"Germany"
```

and:

```text
"Federal Republic of Germany"
```

may be semantically equivalent under one identity contract.

But not necessarily under every contract.

---

# 459.5 Structural equality

### Definition

**Structural equality** means equality of the relevant structural representation.

For example, two JSON objects may have identical fields and values.

$$
A=B
$$

at the representation level.

But:

$$
A=B\not\Rightarrow A\equiv_\Gamma^{sem}B
$$

if the representation is interpreted under different contracts.

Conversely:

$$
A\equiv_\Gamma^{sem}B
$$

does not require:

$$
A=B.
$$

---

# 459.6 Graph isomorphism

Now we introduce a classical mathematical concept.

### Definition

Two graphs are **isomorphic** if there is a bijection between their nodes that preserves the relevant edges.

Let:

$$
G=(V,E)
$$

and:

$$
G'=(V',E').
$$

An isomorphism is a bijection:

$$
f:V\rightarrow V'
$$

such that:

$$
(u,v)\in E
\iff
(f(u),f(v))\in E'.
$$

In simple language:

> The graphs have exactly the same structure, merely with different node names.

---

# 459.7 Example of graph isomorphism

Graph A:

```text
A → B
B → C
```

Graph B:

```text
X → Y
Y → Z
```

The mapping:

$$
f(A)=X,\quad f(B)=Y,\quad f(C)=Z
$$

preserves the structure.

Therefore:

$$
G_A\cong G_B.
$$

---

# 459.8 Isomorphism does not establish semantic equivalence

Now:

```text
A --owns--> B
B --owns--> C
```

versus:

```text
X --reportsTo--> Y
Y --reportsTo--> Z
```

They can be graph-isomorphic as unlabeled graphs.

But they clearly do not mean the same thing.

Thus:

$$
\boxed{
GraphIsomorphism\not\Rightarrow SemanticEquivalence.
}
$$

For KnowledgeOS, this is a fundamental result.

---

# 459.9 Typed graph isomorphism

Suppose the relation types are preserved.

Then we require:

$$
Type_V(v)=Type_{V'}(f(v))
$$

and:

$$
Type_E(e)=Type_{E'}(f(e)).
$$

This is much stronger.

But even typed graph isomorphism is not necessarily semantic equivalence.

Why?

Because semantics include laws.

For example, two relations may both be called:

```text
MemberOf
```

but have different temporal or jurisdictional meanings.

Therefore:

$$
\boxed{
TypedIsomorphism\neq SemanticEquivalence.
}
$$

---

# 459.10 Semantic isomorphism

We can define a stronger concept.

### Definition

A **semantic isomorphism** is a structure-preserving bijection whose mapping also preserves the semantic laws relevant to the contract.

Conceptually:

$$
f:A\cong_\Gamma B
$$

if:

1. identities correspond;
2. relation types correspond;
3. arguments correspond;
4. relevant constraints correspond;
5. transition semantics correspond;
6. interpretation semantics correspond.

Thus:

$$
\boxed{
SemanticIsomorphism
=
StructuralIsomorphism
+
SemanticLawPreservation.
}
$$

This is a useful **derived concept**, not a Kernel primitive.

---

# 459.11 Homomorphism

### Definition

A **homomorphism** is a mapping between structures that preserves specified operations or relations, but need not be bijective.

For graphs:

$$
f:V\rightarrow V'
$$

such that:

$$
(u,v)\in E
\Rightarrow
(f(u),f(v))\in E'.
$$

Unlike isomorphism:

$$
f
$$

need not be one-to-one or onto.

---

# 459.12 Real-world homomorphism

Suppose:

```text
Detailed Organization
├── Team A
├── Team B
├── Team C
└── Team D
```

is mapped to:

```text
Technology Division
```

The detailed structure is compressed into a higher-level representation.

The mapping preserves some organizational relationship but intentionally loses detail.

This is not equality.

It may be a homomorphic abstraction.

Therefore:

$$
\boxed{
Homomorphism\neq Equivalence.
}
$$

---

# 459.13 Homomorphism and KnowledgeOS

This is useful for abstraction.

Suppose:

$$
f:K_{detailed}\rightarrow K_{summary}.
$$

If relevant semantic relations are preserved:

$$
R(x,y)\Rightarrow R'(f(x),f(y)),
$$

then \(f\) may be a semantic homomorphism.

But information may be lost.

Therefore:

$$
HomomorphicTransformation
\Rightarrow
PotentialInformationLoss.
$$

This connects directly to Step 420's memory/compression work.

---

# 459.14 Embedding

### Definition

An **embedding** is a mapping that places one structure inside another while preserving selected structure.

In a mathematical setting:

$$
f:A\hookrightarrow B.
$$

It is generally injective.

In ML, however, "embedding" commonly means a vector representation:

$$
f(x)\in\mathbb R^d.
$$

These two uses must not be confused.

$$
\boxed{
MathematicalEmbedding\neq MLVectorEmbedding.
}
$$

This is an important KnowledgeOS vocabulary distinction.

---

# 459.15 ML embedding

Suppose:

$$
f(\text{"Nexus Repository"})=
(0.12,-0.44,\ldots).
$$

The vector allows similarity calculations:

$$
sim(f(x),f(y)).
$$

It does **not** automatically preserve:

* identity;
* causality;
* authority;
* temporal validity;
* truth;
* provenance.

Therefore:

$$
\boxed{
EmbeddingSimilarity\neq SemanticEquivalence.
}
$$

---

# 459.16 Simulation

### Definition

A **simulation relation** says that one system can reproduce or match the observable behavior of another system under specified observations.

Suppose:

$$
A
$$

is a detailed system and:

$$
B
$$

is an abstraction.

If every behavior of A relevant to the observer can be matched by B, then B may simulate A.

Symbolically:

$$
A\preceq_{\Gamma}^{sim}B.
$$

This is weaker than equality.

---

# 459.17 Example: payment system

System A:

```text
Payment
→ FraudCheck
→ Authorization
→ Settlement
```

System B:

```text
Payment
→ Process
```

For an external observer interested only in:

```text
success / failure
```

B may simulate A.

For an observer interested in:

```text
fraud detection
authorization reason
settlement state
```

B does not preserve sufficient behavior.

Therefore:

$$
\boxed{
Simulation\ is\ observation-relative.
}
$$

---

# 459.18 Observation contract

This introduces an important concept.

### Definition

An **observation contract** specifies what distinctions an observer is permitted or required to observe.

Let:

$$
\mathcal O
$$

be the observation contract.

Then:

$$
A\equiv_{\mathcal O}B
$$

means that permitted observations cannot distinguish A and B.

This connects directly to Step 335's observational equivalence.

---

# 459.19 Observational equivalence

### Definition

Two structures are **observationally equivalent** under \(\mathcal O\) if all permitted observations produce equivalent results.

$$
\boxed{
A\equiv_{\mathcal O}B.
}
$$

This does not imply internal identity.

Example:

Two servers produce exactly the same API responses.

They may still have:

* different hardware;
* different operating systems;
* different internal architectures.

Therefore:

$$
ObservationalEquivalence\neq Identity.
$$

---

# 459.20 Bisimulation

### Definition

**Bisimulation** is a relation between transition systems where each system can match the other's observable transitions.

Informally:

> A can simulate B, and B can simulate A, step by step, under the selected observation semantics.

If:

$$
A\sim_\Gamma B
$$

under bisimulation, then they are behaviorally equivalent under that regime.

---

# 459.21 Why bisimulation matters

Consider:

```text id="8d2qzq"
System A:
State 0 → State 1 → State 2

System B:
State X → State Y → State Z
```

If corresponding transitions and observations match, the internal labels need not matter.

This is valuable for:

* service replacement;
* architecture migration;
* version compatibility;
* workflow equivalence;
* protocol verification.

---

# 459.22 Bisimulation does not imply epistemic equivalence

Two systems may behave identically but have different evidence/provenance.

Example:

```text
Model A
```

and:

```text
Model B
```

produce identical predictions.

But A was trained on verified data while B was trained on synthetic data.

Then:

$$
BehavioralEquivalence
\not\Rightarrow
EpistemicEquivalence.
$$

This is a critical KnowledgeOS result.

---

# 459.23 Refinement

### Definition

A **refinement** is a transformation from a less detailed specification to a more detailed one while preserving the properties required by the original specification.

Symbolically:

$$
A\preceq_\Gamma B
$$

may mean:

> B refines A under contract \(\Gamma\).

Example:

```text id="nqcr0b"
Specification:
Payment must be authorized.
```

refined into:

```text id="t1ikm6"
Check identity
→ Check risk
→ Check authorization
→ Record authorization
```

If the refined system preserves the original requirement, it may be a valid refinement.

---

# 459.24 Refinement is not "more knowledge"

This distinction is essential.

A representation may be more detailed but wrong.

Example:

```text id="d6k9qk"
A: Customer = John
```

B adds:

```text
John's address = 123 Main Street
```

If the address is false, B contains more representation but is not a knowledge refinement.

Therefore:

$$
\boxed{
RepresentationRefinement\neq KnowledgeRefinement.
}
$$

This confirms Step 395.

---

# 459.25 Safe refinement

We can define:

### Safe refinement [PROP]

A refinement is **safe** if it preserves all properties declared non-negotiable by the contract.

$$
SafeRefine_\Gamma(A,B)
$$

requires:

$$
RequiredProperties(A)
\subseteq
PreservedProperties(B).
$$

But "safe" is itself contract-relative.

---

# 459.26 Abstraction

### Definition

**Abstraction** removes or hides distinctions considered irrelevant for a specified purpose.

$$
Abs_\Gamma(A)=B.
$$

Example:

Detailed employee record:

```text
name
address
salary
department
role
age
```

Decision system might use only:

```text
department
role
authorization
```

The abstraction deliberately removes other dimensions.

Therefore:

$$
Abstraction\neq Error.
$$

But:

$$
Abstraction\neq Completeness.
$$

This directly confirms previous Zero principles.

---

# 459.27 Information-preserving transformation

### Definition

A transformation \(T\) is **information-preserving** under observation contract \(\mathcal O\) if:

$$
A\equiv_{\mathcal O}T(A).
$$

That means the transformation does not destroy any distinction relevant to \(\mathcal O\).

It need not preserve every possible distinction.

Thus:

$$
InformationPreservation
$$

is always relative to an observation contract.

---

# 459.28 Lossy transformation

### Definition

A **lossy transformation** removes information or distinctions.

$$
T:A\rightarrow B.
$$

If there exist:

$$
x\neq y
$$

such that:

$$
T(x)=T(y),
$$

then the transformation is non-injective and can collapse distinctions.

Example:

```text id="x3xg3a"
Alice, 35
Bob, 35
```

mapped to:

```text
Age = 35
```

loses identity.

---

# 459.29 Semantic loss

### Definition

**Semantic loss** occurs when a transformation removes distinctions required by a semantic contract.

This is more precise than generic information loss.

For example, removing:

```text
valid_from
valid_to
```

from a governance relation may preserve many fields but destroy temporal semantics.

Therefore:

$$
InformationLoss\neq SemanticLoss.
$$

---

# 459.30 Semantic preservation

### Definition

A transformation is **semantically preserving** under \(\Gamma\) if all semantic properties declared relevant by \(\Gamma\) remain recoverable or observationally equivalent after transformation.

$$
Preserve_\Gamma(T).
$$

This gives us a formal bridge between:

* migration;
* compression;
* schema mapping;
* ML transformation;
* graph projection.

---

# 459.31 Compatibility

### Definition

Two structures are **compatible** under a contract if they can coexist, interoperate or be transformed without violating the contract.

$$
Compatible_\Gamma(A,B).
$$

Compatibility is weaker than equivalence.

For example:

```text
System A: REST API
System B: GraphQL API
```

may be compatible through an adapter even though they are not structurally equivalent.

---

# 459.32 Incompatibility

### Definition

Two structures are incompatible under \(\Gamma\) if their combination or interaction violates a declared requirement.

$$
\neg Compatible_\Gamma(A,B).
$$

Example:

System A requires:

$$
TLS1.3
$$

System B only supports:

$$
TLS1.0.
$$

Under a TLS1.3-only security contract:

$$
Incompatible_\Gamma(A,B).
$$

But outside that contract they may be interoperable.

Thus:

$$
\boxed{
Compatibility\ is\ contract-relative.
}
$$

---

# 459.33 Schema compatibility

### Definition

**Schema compatibility** means two schemas can interoperate or map without violating specified structural constraints.

There are several levels:

* backward compatibility;
* forward compatibility;
* read compatibility;
* write compatibility;
* semantic compatibility.

These must not be collapsed.

---

# 459.34 Backward compatibility

A new system can consume artifacts produced by an older system.

$$
NewReader(OldData)=Valid.
$$

### Forward compatibility

An older system can consume artifacts produced by a newer system, perhaps ignoring unknown fields.

$$
OldReader(NewData)=Valid.
$$

These are operational properties, not universal semantic equivalence.

---

# 459.35 Schema evolution

### Definition

**Schema evolution** is controlled change to the structure/schema while preserving declared compatibility properties.

Example:

Version 1:

```text
Customer:
  name
```

Version 2:

```text
Customer:
  name
  email
```

This may be backward-compatible.

But changing:

```text
customer_id
```

from integer to a different semantic identifier may not be.

Therefore:

$$
FieldChange\neq SemanticNeutrality.
$$

---

# 459.36 Ontology alignment

### Definition

**Ontology alignment** is the process of establishing correspondences between concepts/relations in different semantic models.

Example:

Ontology A:

$$
Customer
$$

Ontology B:

$$
Client.
$$

An alignment hypothesis may be:

$$
Customer_A\equiv Client_B.
$$

But this is not automatically true.

It requires evidence and semantic analysis.

Thus:

$$
OntologyAlignment\subseteq Correspondence.
$$

No new Kernel primitive is required.

---

# 459.37 Correspondence versus equivalence

This distinction is crucial.

Correspondence says:

> These structures are related under some mapping hypothesis.

Equivalence says:

> They satisfy a stronger contract of interchangeability or indistinguishability.

Therefore:

$$
\boxed{
Correspondence\neq Equivalence.
}
$$

This strengthens Step 455.

---

# 459.38 Mapping hypothesis

### Definition

A **mapping hypothesis** is a candidate assertion that one representation corresponds to another.

Example:

$$
Customer_A\leftrightarrow Client_B.
$$

It may have:

* confidence;
* evidence;
* provenance;
* temporal validity;
* semantic contract.

Therefore it can be represented as an ordinary relation instance.

---

# 459.39 Mapping validation

### Definition

**Mapping validation** is the evaluation of whether a candidate mapping satisfies its declared semantic contract.

For candidate \(m\):

$$
Eval_\Gamma(m).
$$

Possible output:

$$
Valid,\ Invalid,\ Unknown
$$

or richer values.

This connects mapping to Gate B.

---

# 459.40 Structure transformation

### Definition

A **structure transformation** maps one representation or semantic structure into another:

$$
T:A\rightarrow B.
$$

Examples:

* database migration;
* JSON → relational;
* ontology → taxonomy;
* detailed graph → summary graph;
* legacy API → new API;
* event history → current state.

The crucial question is:

$$
\boxed{
What properties does T preserve?
}
$$

---

# 459.41 Transformation contract

### Definition

A **transformation contract** specifies:

1. source structure;
2. target structure;
3. preserved properties;
4. allowed losses;
5. assumptions;
6. error conditions;
7. provenance requirements;
8. reversibility requirements.

We can write:

$$
TC=(Source,Target,Preserve,Loss,Assumptions,Validation).
$$

This is an application/semantic contract, not a Kernel primitive.

---

# 459.42 Reversibility

### Definition

A transformation is **reversible** if the original relevant structure can be reconstructed from the transformed structure.

Strictly:

$$
T^{-1}(T(x))=x.
$$

But KnowledgeOS should use a weaker practical notion:

$$
Recover_\Gamma(T(x))\equiv_\Gamma x.
$$

That is:

> exact physical recovery is unnecessary if all contract-relevant semantics remain recoverable.

---

# 459.43 Lossless transformation

### Definition

A transformation is **lossless under \(\Gamma\)** if:

$$
T(x)\equiv_\Gamma x.
$$

This is stronger than merely "we can approximately reconstruct it."

---

# 459.44 Approximate transformation

### Definition

An **approximate transformation** preserves only selected properties within declared error bounds.

For example:

$$
d_\Gamma(A,T(A))\le\epsilon
$$

under a specified metric \(d_\Gamma\).

But we already know from Step 396:

$$
\text{No universal epistemic metric exists.}
$$

Therefore this metric must be regime-specific.

---

# 459.45 Semantic loss budget

We previously marked this as [PROP].

A **semantic loss budget** specifies which semantic distinctions may be lost and to what extent.

For example:

```text id="gk3yqn"
Allowed:
  formatting differences

Forbidden:
  identity differences
  authorization differences
  temporal-validity differences
```

This is extremely useful for real-world migration.

But it remains a contract-level concept.

---

# 459.46 Structural equivalence matrix

We can now construct a useful conceptual matrix.

| Relation between A and B |                   Same? | Information preserved? | Typical use          |
| ------------------------ | ----------------------: | ---------------------: | -------------------- |
| Representation equality  |                     Yes |                    Yes | exact copy           |
| Structural isomorphism   |            structurally |                usually | graph equivalence    |
| Semantic isomorphism     |            semantically |                    Yes | migration            |
| Homomorphism             |                      No |               selected | abstraction          |
| Simulation               |                      No |           behaviorally | system replacement   |
| Bisimulation             | behaviorally equivalent |               selected | protocol equivalence |
| Refinement               |                      No |    required properties | implementation       |
| Abstraction              |                      No |               selected | summarization        |
| Correspondence           |                 unknown |                unknown | matching             |
| Compatibility            |                      No | enough to interoperate | integration          |
| Semantic equivalence     |          under contract |     relevant semantics | interchangeability   |

This is a very useful conceptual separation for KnowledgeOS.

# 459.47 Can these concepts be Kernel primitives?

Let's attack them.

### Graph Isomorphism

Requires graph structure and mapping.

Graph is derived.

**No primitive.**

### Homomorphism

A mapping over relations.

**No primitive.**

### Simulation

Transition semantics + observation contract.

Already represented.

**No primitive.**

### Bisimulation

Transition semantics + relation between states.

**No primitive.**

### Refinement

Semantic relation between structures.

**No primitive.**

### Compatibility

Contract evaluation.

**No primitive.**

### Transformation

Relation/process with semantic contract.

**No primitive.**

### Equivalence

Semantic relation.

**No primitive.**

### Mapping

Typed relation instance.

**No primitive.**

### Alignment

Collection of correspondence relations.

**No primitive.**

Again:

$$
\boxed{
ID+\mathcal R^\star+\mathsf{Sem}
}
$$

survives.

# 459.48 A deeper mathematical insight

We now have three increasingly strong notions:

$$
\boxed{
Correspondence
\rightarrow
Structural Preservation
\rightarrow
Semantic Preservation
}
$$

A candidate mapping may first establish:

$$
Correspondence(A,B).
$$

Then:

$$
StructurePreserved_\Gamma(A,B).
$$

Then potentially:

$$
SemanticEquivalent_\Gamma(A,B).
$$

These must not be collapsed.

This provides a disciplined pipeline for schema/ontology migration.

# 459.49 ML-assisted equivalence detection

ML can help generate candidate mappings.

For example:

$$
Embedding(Customer)\approx Embedding(Client).
$$

Then:

```text id="v4a6d7"
CandidateMapping(Customer, Client)
```

is generated.

Next:

### Structural check

Do their relations match?

### Constraint check

Do their cardinalities match?

### Temporal check

Do validity semantics match?

### Identity check

Do identity criteria match?

### Behavioral check

Do transitions match?

### Provenance check

Are sources sufficiently independent?

Only after these checks can we classify:

$$
Equivalent,\ Compatible,\ Refinement,\ Corresponding,\ Incompatible,\ Unknown.
$$

This is an excellent example of ML serving epistemic reasoning rather than replacing it.

# 459.50 Example: database migration

Suppose legacy database:

```text id="j7v85r"
PERSON
------
PERSON_ID
NAME

EMPLOYEE
--------
EMPLOYEE_ID
PERSON_ID
DEPARTMENT_ID
```

New database:

```text id="jq4yqg"
Employee
-------
id
name
department
```

A naive schema matcher might conclude:

$$
PERSON\leftrightarrow Employee.
$$

But that may be false.

The legacy model distinguishes:

$$
Person
$$

from:

$$
Employee.
$$

The new model collapses them.

This is a semantic loss.

KnowledgeOS should detect:

$$
\boxed{
EntityCollapse
}
$$

and ask whether that collapse is permitted.

# 459.51 Example: organizational migration

Legacy:

```text
Department
  └── Employee
```

New:

```text
Division
  └── Team
       └── Employee
```

The new system is not necessarily equivalent.

It may be a refinement:

$$
LegacyStructure
\preceq
NewStructure.
$$

But only if:

$$
Department
$$

can safely be represented by:

$$
Division/Team.
$$

That requires a transformation contract.

# 459.52 Example: KnowledgeOS decision model

Suppose two decision models:

### Model A

$$
Cost + Security + Availability.
$$

### Model B

$$
Cost + Security + Availability + Skills.
$$

B is structurally richer.

Is B better?

Not automatically.

If `Skills` is badly measured, it could introduce noise.

Therefore:

$$
ModelRefinement\neq DecisionImprovement.
$$

Again:

$$
MoreStructure\neq MoreKnowledge.
$$

# 459.53 Cross-graph equivalence

Suppose:

$$
G_A=GovernanceGraph
$$

and:

$$
G_B=DecisionGraph.
$$

Can they be equivalent?

Generally no.

They have different semantic purposes.

But a projection may preserve a selected subset:

$$
\Pi_{auth}(G_A)
\cong_\Gamma
\Pi_{auth}(G_B).
$$

Therefore equivalence should often be checked **after selecting a semantic projection**.

This is a major architectural insight.

---

# 459.54 Projection-relative equivalence

Define:

$$
\boxed{
A\equiv_{\Gamma,\Pi}B
}
$$

to mean:

$$
\Pi_\Gamma(A)\equiv_\Gamma\Pi_\Gamma(B).
$$

This allows:

* operational equivalence;
* decision equivalence;
* governance equivalence;
* observational equivalence.

without requiring entire structures to be identical.

This is much more realistic.

---

# 459.55 Equivalence is purpose-relative

The same two structures can be:

$$
A\equiv_{\mathcal O_1}B
$$

but:

$$
A\not\equiv_{\mathcal O_2}B.
$$

Example:

Two cloud deployment architectures produce identical application behavior.

For an application user:

$$
A\equiv B.
$$

For an infrastructure operator concerned with recovery:

$$
A\not\equiv B.
$$

Therefore:

$$
\boxed{
Equivalence\ requires\ an\ observation/semantic\ contract.
}
$$

# 459.56 DDD consequence: anti-corruption layer

This directly connects to a classic DDD pattern: the **Anti-Corruption Layer**.

An Anti-Corruption Layer protects one domain model from semantic contamination by another model.
