# KnowledgeOS — Step 461

## Invariance, Variation, Noise, Error, Anomaly, Corruption, Tampering, Adversarial Change, Unauthorized Change, Fault, Failure, Legitimate Evolution, Change Validation, Change Attribution, Change Authorization, Structural Integrity, Semantic Integrity and Trustworthy Change Detection

We continue from Step 460.

Step 460 established that a difference can occur at several levels:

$$
\Delta=
(\Delta_I,\Delta_R,\Delta_S,\Delta_M,\Delta_B,\Delta_\Gamma,\Delta_Q)
$$

for identity, representation, structure, semantics, behavior, contract and inquiry/requirements.

But a new problem appears:

> **Not every observed difference is a meaningful change.**

A measured value can change because of noise.

A database record can change because of corruption.

A policy can change because someone edited it.

A system can change because an authorized release was deployed.

A model can change because of retraining.

An apparent anomaly can simply be a rare but legitimate event.

An ML system can classify an authorized change as an attack.

Therefore we need to distinguish:

$$
\boxed{
ObservedDifference
\neq
MeaningfulChange
\neq
LegitimateChange
\neq
AuthorizedChange
}
$$

This step investigates whether these distinctions require new KnowledgeOS primitives.

The preliminary answer is again **no**, but the analysis reveals an important new architecture: **Change Assurance** must become a first-class capability, while remaining outside the Kernel.

---

# 461.1 Observation

### Definition

An **observation** is an obtained representation of some phenomenon through an observation process.

$$
O_t=Observe(X_t,\mathcal O,\Gamma).
$$

The observation may come from:

* sensor;
* database;
* log;
* human report;
* API;
* document;
* ML model;
* measurement instrument.

Observation is not necessarily the phenomenon itself.

$$
\boxed{
Observation\neq Reality.
}
$$

This is already a KnowledgeOS invariant.

---

# 461.2 Observed difference

### Definition

An **observed difference** exists when two observations differ under a comparison function:

$$
Diff_\Gamma(O_t,O_{t+1})\neq\varnothing.
$$

Example:

```text
09:00 CPU = 40%
09:01 CPU = 70%
```

There is an observed difference.

But we do not yet know whether:

* workload increased;
* sensor malfunctioned;
* attack occurred;
* measurement noise occurred.

Therefore:

$$
ObservedDifference\not\Rightarrow Explanation.
$$

---

# 461.3 Variation

### Definition

**Variation** is a difference occurring within the range of behavior considered normal or expected under a specified model.

For random variable \(X\):

$$
X_t\sim P_\Gamma(X).
$$

A change in \(X_t\) does not necessarily indicate a new regime.

Example:

$$
CPU_t=40\%,42\%,39\%,45\%.
$$

This is ordinary variation.

Thus:

$$
Variation\neq Drift.
$$

---

# 461.4 Noise

### Definition

**Noise** is unwanted or irrelevant variation introduced by the observation, measurement or communication process relative to a declared purpose.

A simple measurement model is:

$$
Y=X+\epsilon
$$

where:

* \(X\) = underlying quantity;
* \(Y\) = observed value;
* \(\epsilon\) = noise.

Noise may be:

* random;
* systematic;
* temporal;
* environmental;
* instrument-specific.

### Critical point

Noise is not necessarily false data.

It is a component of the observation process.

Therefore:

$$
Noise\neq Error.
$$

---

# 461.5 Error

### Definition

An **error** is a deviation between an observed/represented/computed result and a reference or intended result under a specified contract.

$$
Error_\Gamma(Y,Y^*)=d_\Gamma(Y,Y^*).
$$

The reference \(Y^*\) might be:

* ground truth;
* specification;
* expected value;
* reference implementation;
* calibrated measurement.

Error therefore requires a reference.

Without a reference:

$$
Difference\neq Error.
$$

---

# 461.6 Noise versus error

Consider a thermometer.

True temperature:

$$
X=20^\circ C.
$$

Measurements:

$$
19.9,\ 20.2,\ 20.1,\ 19.8.
$$

Variation around 20°C may be measurement noise.

If the thermometer consistently reports:

$$
25^\circ C
$$

because it is miscalibrated, that is systematic measurement error.

Thus:

$$
Noise\rightarrow ObservationVariation
$$

while:

$$
Error\rightarrow ReferenceDeviation.
$$

They can coexist.

---

# 461.7 Bias

### Definition

**Bias** is systematic deviation of an estimator or measurement procedure from its target.

For estimator \(\hat\theta\):

$$
Bias(\hat\theta)=E[\hat\theta]-\theta.
$$

An estimator can be:

* noisy but unbiased;
* precise but biased;
* both;
* neither.

This is why:

$$
Precision\neq Accuracy.
$$

And:

$$
LowVariance\neq Correctness.
$$

---

# 461.8 Accuracy

### Definition

**Accuracy** describes closeness to the relevant reference value.

It is contract-dependent because the reference must be defined.

For classification:

$$
Accuracy=
\frac{\text{correct predictions}}{\text{all predictions}}.
$$

But accuracy alone can be misleading with imbalanced data.

Example:

If 99% of cases are legitimate, predicting "legitimate" always gives:

$$
Accuracy=99\%.
$$

Yet fraud detection is useless.

Therefore:

$$
Accuracy\neq Adequacy.
$$

---

# 461.9 Precision

Two meanings must be separated.

### Statistical measurement precision

Low variability among repeated measurements.

### ML classification precision

$$
Precision=\frac{TP}{TP+FP}.
$$

These are different concepts.

KnowledgeOS should never use an unqualified `precision` term when semantic precision matters.

This reinforces the vocabulary discipline.

---

# 461.10 Invariance

Now we reach a central mathematical concept.

### Definition

A property \(P\) is **invariant** under transformation \(T\) if:

$$
P(x)\Rightarrow P(T(x)).
$$

More strongly:

$$
P(T(x))=P(x).
$$

Example:

Changing a database column order should not change the semantic identity of an entity.

If:

$$
T=ColumnReordering
$$

and:

$$
Identity(T(x))=Identity(x),
$$

then identity is invariant under that transformation.

---

# 461.11 Invariance is always relative to a transformation

There is no meaningful universal statement:

> "The system is invariant."

We need:

$$
Invariant_\Gamma(P,T).
$$

Example:

Identity may be invariant under:

$$
RenameField
$$

but not under:

$$
MergeTwoLegalEntities.
$$

Therefore:

$$
\boxed{
Invariance\ is\ transformation-relative.
}
$$

---

# 461.12 Invariant versus unchanged

These are different.

### Unchanged

A particular value happens not to change.

### Invariant

A property is guaranteed/preserved under a class of transformations.

Suppose:

$$
Salary_t=70000
$$

and:

$$
Salary_{t+1}=70000.
$$

This does not prove salary is an invariant.

It simply did not change in this instance.

Thus:

$$
Unchanged\neq Invariant.
$$

---

# 461.13 Structural invariant

A **structural invariant** is a property that must remain true under a specified class of structural transformations.

Example:

$$
ReportsTo(x,x)=False.
$$

If a restructuring preserves this condition, it is structurally invariant.

Another:

$$
EveryRoleAssignment\rightarrow ExistingParticipant.
$$

This can be tested after migration.

---

# 461.14 Semantic invariant

A **semantic invariant** is a meaning/property that remains preserved under transformation.

Example:

Legacy:

```text
customer_id = 123
```

New:

```text
customer_uuid = abc...
```

If the identity mapping is correct:

$$
SemanticIdentity_{old}
=
SemanticIdentity_{new}.
$$

Then identity is semantically invariant under the migration.

---

# 461.15 Behavioral invariant

A **behavioral invariant** is a behavior/property preserved across transformations.

Example:

A payment system migration preserves:

$$
ValidPayment\rightarrow AuthorizedPayment
$$

for all tested admissible cases.

This is stronger than identical implementation.

---

# 461.16 Integrity

### Definition

**Integrity** means preservation of declared properties of a representation, structure or process.

This is not a single universal property.

We should distinguish:

* data integrity;
* structural integrity;
* semantic integrity;
* temporal integrity;
* provenance integrity;
* identity integrity.

---

# 461.17 Structural integrity

### Definition

**Structural integrity** means that a structure satisfies its declared structural constraints.

For example:

$$
PartOf(x,y)
$$

must reference existing entities.

Or:

$$
ReportsTo(x,x)=False.
$$

Structural integrity can be checked using deterministic constraints.

---

# 461.18 Semantic integrity

### Definition

**Semantic integrity** means that the representation continues to satisfy the intended meaning/semantic contract.

Example:

A migration preserves the distinction:

$$
Employee\neq Contractor.
$$

If the new system collapses them into `Person`, semantic integrity may be violated even though all database constraints pass.

Thus:

$$
\boxed{
DatabaseIntegrity\neq SemanticIntegrity.
}
$$

This is extremely important for KnowledgeOS.

---

# 461.19 Provenance integrity

### Definition

**Provenance integrity** means that origin and transformation lineage remain trustworthy and reconstructible.

Example:

```text
Source document
     ↓
Extraction
     ↓
ML classification
     ↓
KnowledgeOS relation
```

If the source link disappears, the derived claim may still exist but provenance integrity has been damaged.

---

# 461.20 Temporal integrity

### Definition

**Temporal integrity** means preservation of valid temporal relationships.

Example:

A policy that became effective on:

$$
2026-06-01
$$

must not be represented as though it were effective in:

$$
2025.
$$

Thus:

$$
TemporalIntegrity\neq DataIntegrity.
$$

---

# 461.21 Anomaly

### Definition

An **anomaly** is an observation or structure that deviates from an expected pattern under a specified model.

$$
Anomaly_\Gamma(x)
$$

may be based on:

* probability;
* distance;
* density;
* rules;
* graph structure;
* temporal behavior.

Example:

An employee normally logs in from Germany, then suddenly logs in from another continent within minutes.

This is anomalous.

But:

$$
Anomaly\neq Error.
$$

The employee may simply be travelling.

---

# 461.22 Outlier

### Definition

An **outlier** is an observation unusually distant from other observations under a specified statistical representation.

For example:

$$
x=1000
$$

when most values lie near:

$$
10-20.
$$

An outlier can be:

* valid;
* erroneous;
* malicious;
* rare.

Therefore:

$$
\boxed{
Outlier\neq Error.
}
$$

---

# 461.23 Anomaly versus outlier

An outlier is generally statistical.

An anomaly can be:

* statistical;
* structural;
* semantic;
* temporal;
* behavioral;
* security-related.

Thus:

$$
Outlier\subseteq \text{possible anomaly class}
$$

under some regimes, but they should not be treated as identical.

---

# 461.24 Corruption

### Definition

**Corruption** is unintended alteration or damage of data/representation such that it no longer faithfully represents the intended content.

Example:

Original:

```text
amount = 1000
```

Corrupted:

```text
amount = 10000
```

due to storage or transmission failure.

Corruption is different from ordinary change.

$$
\boxed{
Corruption=UnintendedIntegrityViolation.
}
$$

under an appropriate contract.

---

# 461.25 Tampering

### Definition

**Tampering** means intentional unauthorized alteration of a representation, structure or system.

Example:

Someone modifies:

```text
ApprovalStatus = Approved
```

without authorization.

Tampering therefore involves:

* intentionality;
* unauthorized modification.

But intent may itself be uncertain.

Therefore:

$$
ObservedModification\not\Rightarrow Tampering.
$$

---

# 461.26 Unauthorized change

### Definition

A change is **unauthorized** when it violates the applicable authority/authorization contract.

$$
Authorized_\Gamma(c)=False.
$$

This does not necessarily mean malicious.

Example:

A developer accidentally deploys a production change without the required approval.

It is unauthorized but not necessarily malicious.

Thus:

$$
Unauthorized\neq Malicious.
$$

---

# 461.27 Malicious change

### Definition

A **malicious change** is a change intentionally made to cause unauthorized harm, deception or violation under a relevant threat model.

This is stronger than unauthorized change.

Therefore:

$$
Malicious\Rightarrow Unauthorized
$$

may hold under some organizational security contracts, but the reverse does not.

---

# 461.28 Adversarial change

### Definition

An **adversarial change** is a deliberate modification designed to exploit weaknesses in a system, model or decision process.

Examples:

* adversarial ML input;
* poisoned training data;
* manipulated evidence;
* altered provenance;
* strategic misinformation.

Adversariality concerns the strategic nature of the modification.

Therefore:

$$
Adversarial\neq Anomalous.
$$

---

# 461.29 Attack

### Definition

An **attack** is an intentional attempt to violate, manipulate or exploit a security, integrity, semantic or decision property.

An attack can target:

* data;
* identity;
* provenance;
* model;
* policy;
* evidence;
* decision.

KnowledgeOS should therefore not restrict attack modeling to cybersecurity.

---

# 461.30 Fault

### Definition

A **fault** is an underlying defect, abnormal condition or cause that can produce incorrect behavior.

Example:

```text
Fault:
database disk failure
```

can produce:

```text
Error:
corrupted record
```

which can produce:

```text
Failure:
incorrect decision.
```

Thus:

$$
\boxed{
Fault\rightarrow Error\rightarrow Failure
}
$$

can be a causal chain.

But the three are not identical.

---

# 461.31 Failure

### Definition

A **failure** occurs when a system does not deliver the behavior required by its contract.

Example:

Required:

$$
BackupCompleted=True
$$

Actual:

$$
BackupCompleted=False.
$$

The system has failed relative to that contract.

Failure is therefore:

$$
Failure_\Gamma.
$$

---

# 461.32 Fault, error and failure

The classic distinction is:

$$
Fault
\rightarrow
Error
\rightarrow
Failure.
$$

Example:

```text
Fault:
incorrect configuration

       ↓

Error:
wrong database connection

       ↓

Failure:
application cannot load data
```

But a fault may remain dormant.

Therefore:

$$
Fault\not\Rightarrow ImmediateFailure.
$$

---

# 461.33 Legitimate change

### Definition

A **legitimate change** is a change that satisfies the applicable semantic, operational and governance conditions.

Formally:

$$
Legitimate_\Gamma(c)
$$

requires some relevant contract to be satisfied.

This does not mean the change is necessarily beneficial.

A bad but authorized architectural decision can be legitimate under one governance contract.

Therefore:

$$
Legitimate\neq Optimal.
$$

---

# 461.34 Authorized change

### Definition

A change is **authorized** if the appropriate authority has granted permission for that change under the applicable authorization contract.

$$
Authorized_\Gamma(c).
$$

Authorization is not proof that the change is technically correct.

Thus:

$$
Authorization\neq Correctness.
$$

This repeats our Governance separation.

---

# 461.35 Valid change

A **valid change** is one that satisfies the relevant technical/semantic constraints.

For example:

$$
Valid_\Gamma(c)=True.
$$

A change can be:

| Valid | Authorized | Interpretation                     |
| ----- | ---------- | ---------------------------------- |
| Yes   | Yes        | legitimate candidate               |
| Yes   | No         | technically valid but unauthorized |
| No    | Yes        | authorized but technically invalid |
| No    | No         | invalid and unauthorized           |

This table is extremely important.

---

# 461.36 Correct change

### Definition

A **correct change** is a change that achieves its intended objective under the relevant correctness contract.

Therefore:

$$
Correct\neq Valid\neq Authorized.
$$

A technically valid change can still fail to achieve the intended business purpose.

---

# 461.37 Change validation

### Definition

**Change validation** evaluates whether a change achieves the intended purpose.

$$
Validate_\Gamma(c).
$$

Example:

Nexus is migrated from on-premises to cloud.

Technical deployment succeeds.

But if:

* backup is unavailable;
* operations cannot support it;
* required GitLab integration fails;

then technical deployment success does not establish overall validation.

---

# 461.38 Change verification

### Definition

**Change verification** asks whether the change conforms to its specification.

$$
Verify_\Gamma(c).
$$

Thus:

$$
Verification\rightarrow Specification
$$

while:

$$
Validation\rightarrow IntendedPurpose.
$$

This repeats Step 406.

---

# 461.39 Change assurance

### Definition

**Change assurance** is the structured body of evidence and evaluation supporting confidence that a change is:

* correctly represented;
* semantically preserved;
* technically valid;
* appropriately authorized;
* adequately tested;
* traceable;
* safe within its operational envelope.

It is a composite assurance capability.

It is **not** a Kernel primitive.

---

# 461.40 Change attribution

### Definition

**Change attribution** attempts to determine which source, actor, process or event is responsible for a change.

Possible relations:

$$
CausedBy(c,a)
$$

$$
PerformedBy(c,a)
$$

$$
ApprovedBy(c,a).
$$

These are different.

For example:

```text
Developer performed deployment.
Architecture Board approved it.
Vendor change caused the dependency update.
```

Therefore:

$$
PerformedBy\neq ApprovedBy\neq CausedBy.
$$

---

# 461.41 Change causality

A causal relation asks:

> Did X contribute to producing Y under the relevant causal model?

$$
Cause(X,Y,\Gamma_{causal}).
$$

Causal inference remains external.

We must not infer:

$$
ActorRecordedOnChange\Rightarrow Cause(actor,change).
$$

---

# 461.42 Change authorization

### Definition

**Change authorization** is the governance decision permitting a change.

$$
Authorize(a,c,\Gamma_{gov}).
$$

It depends on:

* authority;
* scope;
* policy;
* conditions;
* time;
* possibly compensating controls.

Again:

$$
Authorization\neq Causation.
$$

---

# 461.43 Change provenance

### Definition

**Change provenance** records where the change came from and how it was generated.

A provenance record can contain:

$$
P_c=
(Source,
Actor,
Process,
Model,
Version,
Timestamp,
Parent,
Evidence,
Contract).
$$

This can be represented through existing relation instances.

---

# 461.44 Change lineage

### Definition

**Change lineage** describes the chain:

$$
c_1\rightarrow c_2\rightarrow c_3.
$$

For example:

```text
Requirement change
      ↓
Architecture change
      ↓
Code change
      ↓
Deployment change
      ↓
Operational outcome
```

This is a projection over history and dependency relations.

---

ClassifyChange_\Gamma(c)

with possible dimensions:

$$

And another dimension:

$$

# 461.46 Why a single change label fails

Suppose an ML model changes prediction from:

$$
0.2
$$

to:

$$
0.8.
$$

What happened?

Possibilities:

* new input;
* normal model variation;
* data drift;
* concept drift;
* retraining;
* model replacement;
* calibration change;
* adversarial input;
* corrupted model;
* semantic contract change.

A field:

```text
changeType = MODEL
```

is inadequate.

KnowledgeOS needs a **change profile**.

---

# 461.47 Change profile

### Definition

A **change profile** is a structured projection describing a change across relevant dimensions.

Candidate:

$$
CP(c)=
(
Representation,
Structure,
Semantics,
Identity,
Behavior,
Contract,
Cause,
Authorization,
Impact,
Confidence
).
$$

This should remain a **projection**, not a new Kernel entity.

# 461.48 Statistical change detection

For a time series:

$$
X_1,\ldots,X_n,
$$

we may test:

$$
H_0:P_1=P_2
$$

against:

$$
H_1:P_1\neq P_2.
$$

Possible methods:

* CUSUM;
* Page-Hinkley;
* Bayesian change-point models;
* likelihood-ratio tests;
* kernel methods;
* distribution-distance methods.

But a statistical rejection of \(H_0\) establishes statistical evidence of distributional change.

It does not identify its semantic cause.

Therefore:

$$
\boxed{
StatisticalChangeDetection\neq SemanticChangeDetection.
}
$$

---

# 461.49 Graph change detection

For graph \(G_t\):

$$
G_t=(V_t,E_t)
$$

and:

$$
G_{t+1}=(V_{t+1},E_{t+1}),
$$

we can calculate:

$$
\Delta E=E_{t+1}\setminus E_t
$$

and:

$$
\Delta E^- =E_t\setminus E_{t+1}.
$$

But graph difference alone cannot determine whether the changes are legitimate.

Example:

```text
ReportsTo(A,B)
```

removed and:

```text
ReportsTo(A,C)
```

added.

This could be:

* promotion;
* reorganization;
* data error;
* unauthorized modification.

Therefore:

$$
GraphDiff\neq ChangeExplanation.
$$

---

# 461.50 ML anomaly detection

ML can estimate:

$$
P(Anomaly\mid X).
$$

Useful methods include:

* isolation forests;
* one-class models;
* autoencoders;
* density estimation;
* graph anomaly detection;
* sequence models.

But:

$$
P(Anomaly)\neq P(Attack).
$$

Anomaly detection is a candidate-generation mechanism.

---

# 461.51 Adversarial ML example

Suppose a classifier receives an input \(x\).

Original:

$$
f(x)=Cat.
$$

Perturbed:

$$
x'=x+\delta
$$

with:

$$
\|\delta\|\le\epsilon
$$

but:

$$
f(x')=Dog.
$$

This is an adversarial example under a specified threat model.

However, not every small perturbation is adversarial.

Thus:

$$
SmallDifference\neq AdversarialAttack.
$$

---

# 461.52 Data poisoning

### Definition

**Data poisoning** is deliberate manipulation of training data to alter model behavior.

Training:

$$
D
$$

becomes:

$$
D'=D\cup D_{poison}.
$$

The resulting model:

$$
M'=Train(D')
$$

may behave differently.

KnowledgeOS should preserve:

$$
TrainingDataVersion
$$

and:

$$
ModelLineage.
$$

Then:

$$
ModelChange
$$

can be traced to its training-data changes.

---

# 461.53 Semantic poisoning

A broader possibility is **semantic poisoning [PROP]**:

> deliberate insertion of misleading semantic content into the epistemic substrate so that downstream interpretation or decisions are distorted.

Example:

A fabricated policy document is inserted into the knowledge base.

It may be linguistically convincing.

But:

$$
DocumentPresence\neq PolicyAuthority.
$$

This connects directly to the Governance layer.

---

# 461.54 Trust

### Definition

**Trust** is a context-dependent degree of reliance placed on a source, process, representation or result.

Trust may depend on:

* reliability;
* provenance;
* historical performance;
* authority;
* calibration;
* security;
* independence.

Trust is therefore not truth.

$$
\boxed{
Trust\neq Truth.
}
$$

---

# 461.55 Trustworthy change

### Definition

A **trustworthy change** is a change for which the available assurance is sufficient for the declared use and governance contract.

It does **not** mean:

> guaranteed correct.

A better formulation is:

$$
Trustworthy_\Gamma(c)
\iff
Assurance_\Gamma(c)\ge Requirement_\Gamma(c)
$$

where the comparison itself is regime-specific.

This is a candidate [PROP], not a universal theorem.

---

# 461.56 Change confidence

A system may assign:

$$
P(ChangeType=c\mid Evidence).
$$

But this is model confidence.

It is not proof.

Therefore:

$$
ChangeConfidence\neq ChangeTruth.
$$

This is another direct extension of our uncertainty research.

---

# 461.57 Evidence required for change classification

Suppose we observe:

$$
Diff(O_t,O_{t+1}).
$$

To classify it as an authorized semantic change, we may need:

1. source evidence;
2. temporal evidence;
3. authority evidence;
4. version evidence;
5. transformation evidence;
6. behavioral evidence.

Then:

$$
Evidence
\rightarrow
Assessment
\rightarrow
ChangeClassification.
$$

This is a natural extension of the existing epistemic pipeline.

---

# 461.58 A four-stage change assurance pipeline

I recommend:

$$
\boxed{
Detect
\rightarrow
Classify
\rightarrow
Validate
\rightarrow
Attribute
}
$$

### Detect

Did something differ?

### Classify

What kind of difference is it?

### Validate

Is the change legitimate/correct/safe under the relevant contracts?

### Attribute

Who/what produced, authorized or caused it?

These must not be collapsed.

---

# 461.59 Add governance as a separate stage

For organizational changes:

$$
Detect
\rightarrow
Classify
\rightarrow
Validate
\rightarrow
GovernanceCheck
\rightarrow
Attribute
$$

where:

$$
GovernanceCheck
$$

determines:

* authorization;
* authority;
* policy applicability;
* exception;
* conditions.

This is especially relevant to the Nexus case.

# 461.60 Nexus example

Suppose Nexus changes from:

```text
On-prem
```

to:

```text
Cloud
```

KnowledgeOS detects:

$$
InfrastructureChange.
$$

It then asks:

### Structural

Did hosting relation change?

$$
HostedOn(Nexus,OnPrem)
\rightarrow
HostedOn(Nexus,Cloud).
$$

### Semantic

Does `Cloud` have the same operational meaning?

### Behavioral

Did performance/security/backup change?

### Governance

Was the migration authorized?

### Operational

Are cloud skills available?

### Assurance

Does the new environment satisfy required controls?

### Decision

Is the architecture still suitable?

The system therefore does not stop at:

> "Nexus changed."

It reconstructs the change's semantic consequences.

---

# 461.61 Authorized change versus beneficial change

Suppose the architecture board approves:

> move Nexus to cloud.

The migration is authorized.

But later:

* cost doubles;
* availability decreases;
* operational capability is insufficient.

Then:

$$
Authorized=True
$$

but:

$$
DecisionQuality
$$

may be poor.

Therefore:

$$
\boxed{
Authorization\neq OutcomeQuality.
}
$$

This is consistent with Step 438:

$$

# 461.73 Change-point versus regime change

A statistical change-point:

$$

$$

indicates evidence that a statistical process changed.

A semantic regime change means:

$$
\Gamma_t\neq\Gamma_{t+1}.
$$

These can coincide but need not.

Example:

A new policy is introduced but no data has yet changed.

Then:

$$
\Gamma_t\neq\Gamma_{t+1}
$$

while:

$$
P_t(X)=P_{t+1}(X).
$$

Therefore:

$$
\boxed{
SemanticRegimeChange\neq StatisticalChangePoint.
}
$$

# 461.74 Invariance testing

We can formulate a practical test.

Given transformation:

$$
T
$$

and property:

$$
P,
$$

test:

$$
P(x)\stackrel{?}{\Longleftrightarrow}P(T(x)).
$$

If yes under the contract:

$$
Invariant_\Gamma(P,T).
$$

Example:

### Migration invariant

$$
Identity_{old}(x)=Identity_{new}(T(x)).
$$

### Security invariant

$$
AuthorizedAction_{old}
\equiv
AuthorizedAction_{new}.
$$

### Behavioral invariant

$$
Behavior_{old}\equiv_{\mathcal O}Behavior_{new}.
$$

This creates a rigorous migration assurance framework.
