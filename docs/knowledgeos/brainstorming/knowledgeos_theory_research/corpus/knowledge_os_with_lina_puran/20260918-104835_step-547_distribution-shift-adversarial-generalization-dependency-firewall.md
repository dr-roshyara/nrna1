# 547.1 What Step 547 is testing

The question is no longer:

> Can ML detect dependencies in a synthetic world?

We already have evidence that it can.

The harder question is:

$$
\boxed{
\text{Can a learned dependency detector generalize when the mechanism generating dependencies changes?}
}
$$

This is the distinction between:

$$
IID\ Generalization
$$

and:

$$
OOD\ Generalization.
$$

---

# 547.2 Define: Distribution

A **Distribution** is the probability law governing how observations are generated.

Write:

$$
P_{train}(X,Y)
$$

for the training distribution and:

$$
P_{test}(X,Y)
$$

for the test distribution.

If:

$$
P_{train}=P_{test},
$$

we are approximately in the ordinary IID setting.

If:

$$
P_{train}\neq P_{test},
$$

we have **distribution shift**.

---

# 547.3 Define: Distribution Shift

**Distribution Shift** occurs when the statistical mechanism generating the deployment data differs from the mechanism represented during training.

$$
\boxed{
P_{train}(X,Y)\neq P_{test}(X,Y)
}
$$

This is extremely important for KnowledgeOS because real-world dependency mechanisms are not fixed.

---

# 547.4 Define: IID

**IID** means:

$$
Independent\ and\ Identically\ Distributed.
$$

Roughly, test cases are generated from the same statistical population as training cases and do not depend on each other.

Many ML benchmarks implicitly assume this.

KnowledgeOS cannot make that assumption universally.

---

# 547.5 Define: OOD

**Out-of-Distribution (OOD)** means that an input comes from a distribution materially different from the distribution represented by training.

For example:

Training:

> reports with common-source dependencies.

Deployment:

> reports with shared transformations.

The input may look superficially similar while the dependency mechanism is different.

---

# 547.6 Define: Generalization

**Generalization** is the ability of a system to perform correctly on previously unseen instances from the target population.

Ordinary ML asks:

$$
Performance_{train}
\rightarrow
Performance_{test}.
$$

KnowledgeOS needs a stronger question:

$$
Performance_{known\ mechanisms}
\rightarrow
Performance_{unknown\ mechanisms}.
$$

---

# 547.7 Define: Mechanism

A **Mechanism** is the structural process generating a dependency.

Examples:

$$
Source\rightarrow Evidence
$$

$$
Dataset\rightarrow Model\rightarrow Evidence
$$

$$
Transformation\rightarrow Evidence
$$

$$
Assumption\rightarrow Determination.
$$

Two datasets may have similar statistical features while being generated by different mechanisms.

# 547.13 Define: Shortcut

A **Shortcut** is a predictive feature that correlates with the target in training data but does not represent the underlying semantic mechanism reliably.

$$

$$

# 547.16 Formal counterexample

Let:

$$
S(x,y)=1
$$

mean high semantic similarity.

Construct:

$$
S(a,b)=1
$$

but:

$$
DependsOn^*(a,b)=0.
$$

Therefore:

$$
\boxed{
S(a,b)\not\Rightarrow DependsOn(a,b).
}
$$

No amount of empirical correlation in a particular training population can turn this implication into a logical theorem.

---

# 547.17 This is computer logic, not merely ML

The distinction is:

$$
A\rightarrow B
$$

versus:

$$
P(B\mid A)\text{ is high}.
$$

The first is a logical implication.

The second is a statistical relationship.

KnowledgeOS must never silently transform:

$$

$$

into:

$$
A\Rightarrow B.
$$

This is exactly the type of semantic cast we have previously prohibited.

\rightsquigarrow

# 547.19 Define: Unsafe Semantic Cast

$$

# 547.21 Define: Distribution-Shift Detection

**Distribution-Shift Detection** attempts to identify whether current observations differ materially from the population or feature distribution on which a model was validated.

It does **not** prove that the model is wrong.

It provides a warning:

$$
ShiftDetected
\rightarrow
Review/Abstain/Degrade.
$$

---

# 547.22 Why shift detection itself cannot be absolute

Suppose:

$$
P_{test}(X)\neq P_{train}(X).
$$

That does not automatically mean:

$$
P(Y\mid X)
$$

has changed.

This is the distinction between covariate shift and other forms of shift.

Therefore:

$$
\boxed{
InputShift\neq ConceptShift.
}
$$

---

# 547.23 Define: Covariate Shift

**Covariate Shift** occurs when:

$$
P_{train}(X)\neq P_{test}(X)
$$

but approximately:

$$
P_{train}(Y\mid X)
=
P_{test}(Y\mid X).
$$

The input population changes while the conditional relationship remains stable.

---

# 547.24 Define: Concept Shift

**Concept Shift** occurs when:

$$
P_{train}(Y\mid X)
\neq
P_{test}(Y\mid X).
$$

For KnowledgeOS, this is more dangerous.

Example:

During training:

> high citation overlap strongly indicates shared provenance.

Later:

> documents are automatically generated from independent templates containing the same citations.

Then the relationship changes.

---

\rightarrow

# 547.38 Ordinary robustness vs adversarial robustness

Ordinary:

$$
Remove(document)
$$

may be a naturally valid perturbation.

Adversarial:

$$
Construct(document)
$$

specifically to trigger a false dependency.

Therefore:

$$
\boxed{

}
$$

Both belong in the perturbation framework, but require different contracts.

# 547.39 Define: Adversarial Perturbation

An **Adversarial Perturbation** is an input modification intentionally designed to induce a specified erroneous or undesirable system behavior while remaining within the declared input domain.

Example:

The ground truth remains:

$$
Independent.
$$

# 547.60 A major theoretical boundary

The experiment gives us a very important proposition:

> **Observational Non-Identifiability Principle [PROP].**

If two dependency-generating worlds produce indistinguishable observable distributions:

$$
P(X\mid D_1)=P(X\mid D_2),
$$

but:

$$
Dependency(D_1)\neq Dependency(D_2),
$$

then no system using only \(X\) can reliably distinguish the two worlds without additional information.

This is much deeper than an ML limitation.

It is an information-theoretic boundary.

---

# 547.61 Proof

Suppose:

$$
P(X\mid D_1)=P(X\mid D_2).
$$

Any classifier:

$$
f(X)
$$

receives identically distributed observations under both worlds.

Therefore there is no observable statistical signal allowing \(f\) to consistently distinguish them.

Hence:

$$
\boxed{
ObservableIndistinguishability
\Rightarrow
NoGuaranteedDependencyRecovery.
}
$$

Additional information is required.

---

We should **not** conclude:

> ML is unsuitable for KnowledgeOS.

The correct conclusion is narrower:

$$
\boxed{
ML\ is\ useful\ for\ candidate\ discovery,
but\ its\ authority\ must\ be\ bounded\ by\ validation,\ scope,\ calibration,\ and\ adversarial/OOD\ assurance.
}
$$

Likewise we should not conclude that embeddings or LLMs are useless.

Their value is now an empirical question under the stronger benchmark.

# Step 548 — Active Dependency Information Acquisition and Value of Information

Instead of asking ML to guess harder, Step 548 should ask:

$$
\boxed{
\text{What additional observation should KnowledgeOS acquire to resolve an unknown dependency?}
}
$$

For example:

```text id="qv3d2f"
Current:
E1 ?→ E2

Possible actions:

A1: inspect build manifest
A2: inspect source document
A3: inspect transformation log
A4: inspect model lineage
A5: ask domain expert
A6: request timestamped provenance
A7: do nothing
```

We then need to calculate or estimate:

$$
VoI(a)
$$

where **Value of Information** means the expected improvement in the quality of the downstream determination obtained by acquiring information through action \(a\).

But we must be extremely careful: VoI is **not** automatically a scalar universal value.

Step 548 should therefore investigate:

$$
InformationGain
\neq
KnowledgeGain
\neq
DeterminationGain
\neq
DecisionValue.
$$

That is likely to be the next important reduction/irreducibility test for the KnowledgeOS theory.
