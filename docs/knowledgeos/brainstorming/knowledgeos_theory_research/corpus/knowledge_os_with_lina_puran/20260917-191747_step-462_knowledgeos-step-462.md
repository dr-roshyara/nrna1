# KnowledgeOS — Step 462

## Competing Change Hypotheses, Root Cause, Causal Attribution, Diagnosis, Fault Trees, Event Trees, Counterfactuals, Abduction, Explanation and Diagnostic Uncertainty

We continue from Step 461.

Step 461 established:

$$
ObservedDifference
\neq MeaningfulChange
\neq LegitimateChange
\neq AuthorizedChange
$$

and that change classification itself can have several competing explanations.

The next fundamental problem is therefore:

$$
\boxed{
ObservedChange\rightarrow ?
}
$$

Suppose we observe:

> Nexus response time increased from 200 ms to 2 seconds.

Possible explanations include:

$$
H_1=\text{higher workload}
$$

$$
H_2=\text{network degradation}
$$

$$
H_3=\text{database degradation}
$$

$$
H_4=\text{Nexus configuration change}
$$

$$
H_5=\text{hardware fault}
$$

$$
H_6=\text{malicious activity}.
$$

The observation itself does not tell us which explanation is correct.

Therefore:

$$
\boxed{
Observation\rightarrow Competing\ Hypotheses
}
$$

rather than:

$$
Observation\rightarrow RootCause.
$$

This step asks whether **diagnosis, explanation and root-cause reasoning require another Kernel primitive**.

The emerging answer is **no**.

---

# 462.1 Diagnosis

### Definition

**Diagnosis** is the process of determining which candidate explanation(s) best account for observed evidence under a specified diagnostic model.

Formally:

$$
Diag_\Gamma(E,O,H)
\rightarrow
A
$$

where:

* \(E\) = evidence;
* \(O\) = observations;
* \(H\) = hypothesis space;
* \(\Gamma\) = diagnostic regime;
* \(A\subseteq H\) = admissible diagnostic determinations.

Notice the direct connection to the existing determination model:

$$
Det_\Gamma(E,Q,H)\rightarrow A.
$$

Therefore diagnosis appears to be a **specialized determination problem**, not a new ontological primitive.

---

# 462.2 Diagnostic hypothesis

### Definition

A **diagnostic hypothesis** is a candidate explanation for an observed condition.

Example:

$$
H_1=DatabaseOverload.
$$

It is not automatically true.

Therefore:

$$
DiagnosticHypothesis\subseteq Hypothesis.
$$

---

# 462.3 Competing explanations

### Definition

**Competing explanations** are hypotheses that provide alternative accounts of the same observation.

Example:

$$
H_1=\text{network failure}
$$

$$
H_2=\text{database failure}
$$

$$
H_3=\text{application regression}.
$$

The system should preserve all candidates until evidence sufficiently discriminates among them.

This directly extends Step 424.

---

# 462.4 Root cause

### Definition

A **root cause** is a cause selected under a causal/diagnostic model as sufficiently upstream and explanatory for the observed problem.

Important:

There is no universal mathematical definition of "root."

A causal graph may be:

$$
A\rightarrow B\rightarrow C\rightarrow D.
$$

Whether \(A\), \(B\), or \(C\) is called the root cause depends on the diagnostic purpose.

Therefore:

$$
\boxed{
RootCause\ is\ model\ and\ purpose\ relative.
}
$$

---

# 462.5 Root cause versus cause

A **cause** is a causal predecessor/contributor under a causal model.

A **root cause** is a selected upstream explanation according to a diagnostic criterion.

Thus:

$$
Cause\neq RootCause.
$$

Example:

```text id="s3l1v5"
Bad configuration
      ↓
Database overload
      ↓
Application timeout
      ↓
User-visible failure
```

All three upstream relationships may be causal.

The diagnostic process may call:

> Bad configuration

the root cause.

---

# 462.6 Contributing cause

### Definition

A **contributing cause** is a factor that increases the likelihood or severity of an outcome without necessarily being sufficient by itself.

Example:

$$
HighLoad + PoorConfiguration\rightarrow Failure.
$$

Neither factor alone may be sufficient.

Therefore:

$$
ContributingCause\neq SoleCause.
$$

This matters greatly in complex systems.

---

# 462.7 Necessary cause

A cause \(C\) is **necessary** for outcome \(Y\) under a model if:

$$
Y\Rightarrow C.
$$

Without \(C\), \(Y\) cannot occur under that model.

But necessity is model-relative.

---

# 462.8 Sufficient cause

A cause \(C\) is **sufficient** for \(Y\) if:

$$
C\Rightarrow Y.
$$

Again, this is contract/model-relative.

---

# 462.9 Necessary versus sufficient

A condition can be:

* necessary but not sufficient;
* sufficient but not necessary;
* both;
* neither.

Example:

A specific database failure might be sufficient for a service outage.

But service outage can also happen from:

* network failure;
* application crash;
* infrastructure failure.

So:

$$
DatabaseFailure\not\text{ necessary}.
$$

This is why causal reasoning cannot be reduced to simple implication.

---

# 462.10 Abduction

### Definition

**Abduction** is inference from observations toward plausible explanations.

The classical structure is:

$$
Rule:\ H\rightarrow E
$$

$$
Observation:\ E
$$

therefore:

$$
Candidate:\ H.
$$

But the conclusion is not deductively guaranteed.

Example:

$$
DatabaseOverload\rightarrow HighLatency
$$

and:

$$
HighLatency.
$$

Therefore:

$$
DatabaseOverload
$$

is a candidate explanation.

Not a fact.

Thus:

$$
\boxed{
Abduction\neq Deduction.
}
$$

---

# 462.11 Abduction versus induction

**Induction** generalizes from observed cases.

Example:

$$
90/100
$$

observed systems with condition \(X\) fail under condition \(Y\).

Induction may support:

$$
P(Y|X)\approx0.9.
$$

Abduction instead asks:

> What could explain this particular observation?

They are different reasoning tasks.

---

# 462.12 Abduction versus causal inference

Abduction generates plausible explanations.

Causal inference asks whether a causal relationship is justified under a causal model.

Therefore:

$$
Abduction\neq CausalInference.
$$

ML systems often perform abductive-like pattern completion without establishing causality.

---

# 462.13 Explanation

### Definition

An **explanation** is a structured account connecting an observation or conclusion to supporting conditions, mechanisms or evidence under an explanation contract.

An explanation may be:

* causal;
* logical;
* statistical;
* mechanistic;
* evidential;
* procedural.

Therefore:

$$
Explanation\neq Cause.
$$

A mathematical proof can explain why a theorem follows without identifying a real-world cause.

---

# 462.14 Explanation versus justification

A **justification** supports why a conclusion is accepted.

An **explanation** describes why/how something occurs or follows.

Example:

> "The model classified this as fraud because features X, Y and Z were influential."

This is an explanation.

> "The classification was accepted because the model passed the validation threshold."

This is justification.

Therefore:

$$
Explanation\neq Justification.
$$

---

# 462.15 Mechanism

### Definition

A **mechanism** is a structured process through which conditions produce changes or outcomes.

Example:

$$
Overload
\rightarrow
QueueGrowth
\rightarrow
Latency
\rightarrow
Timeout.
$$

Mechanism describes the pathway.

It is stronger than simple correlation.

---

# 462.16 Mechanism versus correlation

Suppose:

$$
IceCreamSales
$$

and:

$$
DrowningDeaths
$$

are correlated.

That does not establish:

$$
IceCreamSales\rightarrow Drowning.
$$

A common factor such as temperature may explain both.

Therefore:

$$
Correlation\neq Mechanism.
$$

This continues Step 402.

---

# 462.17 Confounder

### Definition

A **confounder** is a variable that influences both an apparent cause and outcome, creating or distorting an observed association.

Graphically:

$$
X\leftarrow Z\rightarrow Y.
$$

Example:

$$
Temperature\rightarrow IceCreamSales
$$

and:

$$
Temperature\rightarrow Swimming\rightarrow DrowningRisk.
$$

Ignoring \(Z\) can create a misleading causal interpretation.

---

# 462.18 Mediator

### Definition

A **mediator** lies on a causal pathway:

$$
X\rightarrow M\rightarrow Y.
$$

Example:

$$
Deployment
\rightarrow
ConfigurationChange
\rightarrow
Latency.
$$

Configuration is a mediator.

---

# 462.19 Collider

### Definition

A **collider** is a variable influenced by two variables:

$$
X\rightarrow C\leftarrow Y.
$$

Conditioning on \(C\) can create misleading associations between \(X\) and \(Y\).

This matters for ML-based diagnosis because naïve feature selection can introduce collider bias.

---

# 462.20 Fault tree

### Definition

A **fault tree** is a structured representation of how combinations of lower-level failures can produce a top-level undesired event.

Example:

```text id="x7qk2m"
              SERVICE FAILURE
                    |
                   OR
             ┌──────┴──────┐
             ↓             ↓
        DB failure      Network failure
             |
            AND
        ┌────┴────┐
        ↓         ↓
     Disk full  Bad config
```

The logical semantics are external.

The nodes and relations can be represented by KnowledgeOS.

---

# 462.21 AND gate in fault analysis

If:

$$
A\land B\rightarrow F,
$$

both conditions are required.

Example:

$$
DiskFull\land BadRetryPolicy\rightarrow ServiceFailure.
$$

---

# 462.22 OR gate in fault analysis

If:

$$
A\lor B\rightarrow F,
$$

either condition can produce failure.

Example:

$$
NetworkFailure\lor DatabaseFailure
\rightarrow
ServiceFailure.
$$

This connects directly to the earlier logic-gate reduction.

The important result:

$$
FaultTree
$$

does not require a new Kernel primitive.

It is a typed relation structure plus logical semantics.

---

# 462.23 Event tree

### Definition

An **event tree** starts with an initiating event and branches through possible consequences.

Example:

```text id="s8tqbd"
Nexus outage
      |
      +-- Backup available
      |       |
      |       +-- Recovery succeeds
      |       +-- Recovery fails
      |
      +-- Backup unavailable
              |
              +-- Permanent data loss
```

A fault tree reasons:

$$
Failure\leftarrow Causes.
$$

An event tree reasons:

$$
Event\rightarrow Consequences.
$$

Therefore:

$$
FaultTree\neq EventTree.
$$

---

# 462.24 Fault tree versus event tree

|             | Fault Tree           | Event Tree            |
| ----------- | -------------------- | --------------------- |
| Direction   | backward             | forward               |
| Question    | what caused failure? | what can happen next? |
| Starts with | top event            | initiating event      |
| Main use    | diagnosis            | consequence analysis  |

KnowledgeOS can represent both.

---

# 462.25 Failure mode

### Definition

A **failure mode** is a specific way a system/component can fail.

Example:

```text id="7p3w2e"
Database:
  connection timeout
  data corruption
  authentication failure
  disk exhaustion
```

Failure mode is domain-specific.

---

# 462.26 FMEA

**Failure Mode and Effects Analysis (FMEA)** is a systematic method for identifying:

* failure modes;
* causes;
* effects;
* severity;
* occurrence;
* detectability.

A classical risk priority number may be:

$$
RPN=S\times O\times D.
$$

But this is a methodology, not a universal mathematical truth.

KnowledgeOS should represent the assessment rather than hard-code RPN as universal.

---

# 462.27 Fault diagnosis

A diagnostic system may calculate:

$$
P(H_i|E)
$$

for hypotheses \(H_i\).

Example:

$$
P(H_1|E)=0.60
$$

$$
P(H_2|E)=0.30
$$

$$
P(H_3|E)=0.10.
$$

The correct KnowledgeOS result is not necessarily:

> Root cause = H1.

It may be:

$$
\{H_1,H_2,H_3\}
$$

with probabilities and provenance.

This preserves uncertainty.

---

# 462.28 Bayesian diagnosis

Bayes' theorem:

$$
P(H|E)=
\frac{P(E|H)P(H)}
{P(E)}.
$$

For competing hypotheses:

$$
P(H_i|E).
$$

This is useful when probability assumptions are justified.

But Bayesian diagnosis remains one mathematical regime.

---

# 462.29 Likelihood ratio

For two hypotheses:

$$
LR=
\frac{P(E|H_1)}
{P(E|H_2)}.
$$

If:

$$
LR>1,
$$

evidence favors \(H_1\).

If:

$$
LR<1,
$$

evidence favors \(H_2\).

But evidence weighting must account for dependence and applicability, as established in Step 407.

---

# 462.30 Diagnostic evidence dependence

Suppose:

* three monitoring systems;
* all consume the same underlying database metric.

They all report:

> Database latency increased.

Naively treating these as three independent observations is double counting.

Therefore:

$$
EvidenceCount\neq EvidenceStrength.
$$

KnowledgeOS should preserve source lineage.

---

# 462.31 Diagnostic discrimination

### Definition

**Diagnostic discrimination** is the ability of evidence to distinguish among competing hypotheses.

For:

$$
H_1,H_2,H_3,
$$

evidence is useful if:

$$
P(E|H_i)
$$

differs meaningfully across hypotheses.

If:

$$
P(E|H_1)=P(E|H_2),
$$

then \(E\) does not discriminate between them.

This is closely related to information acquisition.

---

# 462.32 Diagnostic test

A **diagnostic test** is an observation or procedure designed to distinguish candidate explanations.

Example:

Hypotheses:

$$
H_1=NetworkProblem
$$

$$
H_2=DatabaseProblem.
$$

Run:

```text id="z2f7yl"
Database-local latency test.
```

If local latency is normal while external latency is high, the evidence favors \(H_1\).

This is:

$$
HypothesisDiscrimination.
$$

---

# 462.33 Value of a diagnostic test

A test can be evaluated by:

$$
VOI(Test)
$$

or expected information gain.

But:

$$
InformationGain\neq DecisionValue.
$$

A highly informative test may have no effect on the eventual decision.

Conversely, a small amount of information can be decision-critical.

---

# 462.34 Counterfactual diagnosis

### Definition

**Counterfactual diagnosis** asks:

> Would the observed outcome have occurred if a candidate cause had been absent or changed?

Symbolically:

$$
Y_{do(X=x)}
$$

or:

$$
Y_{X\leftarrow x}.
$$

Example:

> Would the latency problem still occur if the new configuration had not been deployed?

This is much stronger than correlation.

---

# 462.35 Counterfactual explanation

In ML, a **counterfactual explanation** identifies a minimal change to inputs that would alter a model's output.

Example:

Model says:

$$
Loan=Reject.
$$

Counterfactual:

> If income were €5,000 higher, the model would approve.

This explains the model's decision behavior.

But:

$$
MLCounterfactual\neq RealWorldCausalCounterfactual.
$$

Unless causal assumptions justify the interpretation.

This distinction is essential.

---

# 462.36 Root-cause ambiguity

Suppose:

$$
E
$$

is compatible with:

$$
H_1,H_2,H_3.
$$

Then:

$$
Det(E)=\{H_1,H_2,H_3\}.
$$

The correct result is:

$$
MultipleDeterminations.
$$

Not:

$$
RootCause=H_1
$$

simply because H1 has the highest score.

This is one of the most important KnowledgeOS principles.

---

# 462.37 Most likely explanation versus determined explanation

Suppose:

$$
P(H_1|E)=0.55
$$

$$
P(H_2|E)=0.45.
$$

It is legitimate to say:

> \(H_1\) is more probable under the model.

It is not necessarily legitimate to say:

> \(H_1\) has been established as the cause.

Therefore:

$$
\boxed{
MostLikely\neq Determined.
}
$$

---

# 462.38 Diagnostic abstention

### Definition

**Diagnostic abstention** means explicitly declining to select a single explanation because available evidence is insufficient.

Example:

$$
P(H_1)=0.51,\quad P(H_2)=0.49.
$$

If the decision is safety-critical, the correct output may be:

$$
Abstain.
$$

This is a strength, not a failure.

---

# 462.39 Diagnostic plurality

KnowledgeOS should support:

$$
A=\{H_1,H_2\}.
$$

This is consistent with:

$$
Det_\Gamma(E,Q,H)\rightarrow\mathcal P(H).
$$

The architecture should preserve plurality all the way into decision analysis.

---

# 462.40 Decision under diagnostic plurality

Suppose:

$$
H_1,H_2
$$

remain plausible.

If both imply the same action:

$$
H_1,H_2\Rightarrow d,
$$

then the system may still recommend:

$$
d.
$$

This is decision robustness.

If:

$$
H_1\Rightarrow d_1
$$

and:

$$
H_2\Rightarrow d_2,
$$

then:

$$
Plurality
\rightarrow
Sensitivity
\rightarrow
VOI
\rightarrow
InformationAcquisition.
$$

This is exactly the KnowledgeOS loop we want.

---

# 462.41 Diagnostic robustness

### Definition

A decision is **diagnostically robust** if it remains acceptable across the relevant surviving diagnostic hypotheses.

Formally, for candidate set \(A\):

$$
Robust(d,A)
$$

if the decision satisfies the declared robustness criterion across \(H\in A\).

For example:

$$
d^*
=
\arg\min_d\max_{H\in A}L(d,H).
$$

This is a minimax-style regime.

---

# 462.42 Root cause and intervention

Identifying a root cause is not sufficient.

We also need:

$$
Intervention.
$$

Example:

$$
BadConfiguration
$$

is diagnosed.

Possible interventions:

* correct configuration;
* replace component;
* add monitoring;
* rollback;
* change architecture.

Therefore:

$$
Diagnosis\neq Intervention.
$$

---

# 462.43 Diagnosis-to-action pipeline

The full pipeline becomes:

$$
Observation
\rightarrow
Anomaly/Change
\rightarrow
HypothesisGeneration
\rightarrow
EvidenceAcquisition
\rightarrow
Diagnosis
\rightarrow
Determination
\rightarrow
Decision
\rightarrow
Authorization
\rightarrow
Action.
$$

This is highly consistent with the existing KnowledgeOS lifecycle.

# 462.44 ML hypothesis generation

ML can generate diagnostic candidates from:

* historical incidents;
* logs;
* traces;
* metrics;
* graph dependencies;
* textual incident reports;
* configuration changes.

For example, an LLM might generate:

```text id="3td9x8"
H1: Database overload
H2: Network congestion
H3: Connection-pool exhaustion
H4: Configuration regression
```

This is useful.

But:

$$
LLM\ hypothesis\ generation
\neq
Diagnosis.
$$

---

# 462.45 ML evidence retrieval

For each hypothesis:

$$
H_i,
$$

the system retrieves relevant evidence:

$$
Retrieve(E|H_i).
$$

This can use:

* BM25;
* embeddings;
* graph traversal;
* temporal filtering;
* source reliability;
* semantic matching.

Retrieval should maximize candidate recall.

---

# 462.46 ML evidence assessment

An ML model can estimate:

$$
Relevance(E,H)
$$

or:

$$
Similarity(E,H).
$$

But:

$$
Similarity\neq EvidenceStrength.
$$

Evidence assessment still needs the explicit epistemic regime.

---

# 462.47 ML causal discovery

ML can search for candidate structures:

$$
X\rightarrow Y.
$$

Examples:

* NOTEARS-style methods;
* constraint-based causal discovery;
* score-based methods;
* temporal causal models.

But causal discovery depends on assumptions such as:

* causal sufficiency;
* faithfulness;
* temporal ordering;
* no hidden confounders.

Therefore:

$$
CausalDiscoveryResult
$$

must carry its assumptions.

---

# 462.48 Causal model provenance

Every causal conclusion should preserve:

$$
ModelID
$$

$$
ModelVersion
$$

$$
AssumptionSet
$$

$$
DataVersion
$$

$$
Method
$$

$$
Timestamp
$$

$$
Evidence.
$$

Otherwise future reassessment cannot distinguish:

> what the system knew

from:

> what the model assumed.

---

# 462.49 Explanation provenance

A generated explanation should preserve:

$$
ExplanationSource.
$$

For an LLM-generated explanation:

```text id="4j35xq"
Explanation:
LLM vX
Prompt/Context:
...
Evidence:
...
Model:
...
Timestamp:
...
```

The explanation itself is a generated artifact.

It should not silently become evidence.

Thus:

$$
\boxed{
GeneratedExplanation\neq IndependentEvidence.
}
$$

---

# 462.50 Explanation hallucination

An **explanation hallucination** occurs when a model generates a plausible explanation that is unsupported by available evidence.

Example:

> "The outage was caused by database overload."

But the evidence only shows:

$$
HighLatency.
$$

No database evidence exists.

Then:

$$
Explanation\not\Rightarrow Evidence.
$$

This is a major reason KnowledgeOS needs independent evidence grounding.

---

# 462.51 Explanation validation

A generated explanation should therefore pass:

$$
Explanation
\rightarrow
EvidenceGrounding
\rightarrow
ConsistencyCheck
\rightarrow
CausalValidity
\rightarrow
Human/AuthorityReview
$$

where appropriate.

# 462.52 Diagnostic argument graph

We can represent:

```text id="m7r3v0"
Observation
   │
   ├── supports → H1
   ├── weakly supports → H2
   └── contradicts → H3
                    │
                    ↓
               Determination
```

This is an ordinary relation graph.

It is not a new epistemic primitive.

---

# 462.53 Defeater in diagnosis

Suppose:

$$
H_1=\text{database failure}.
$$

Evidence:

$$
E_1=\text{database latency high}.
$$

But new evidence:

$$
E_2=\text{database latency was caused by monitoring query}.
$$

Then \(E_2\) can defeat the original inference.

Thus:

$$
Support(H_1)
$$

is reduced by a defeater.

This connects directly to Step 423.

---

# 462.54 Diagnostic defeater graph

A practical graph:

```text id="q8q4bp"
Evidence E1
   ↓
supports
   ↓
H1: Database failure
   ↑
defeated by
   │
Evidence E2
```

KnowledgeOS preserves both.

It does not delete \(H_1\) historically.

---

# 462.55 Root cause can change

At:

$$
t_1:
$$

$$
H_1
$$

may be the leading diagnosis.

At:

$$
t_2:
$$

new evidence may support:

$$
H_2.
$$

Thus:

$$
Diagnosis_{t_1}\neq Diagnosis_{t_2}.
$$

This is epistemic revision, not historical erasure.

---

# 462.56 Diagnostic history

We can reconstruct:

$$
D_t=Derive(H_{\le t},\Gamma_t).
$$

Therefore:

> Why did KnowledgeOS believe H1 at 10:00?

can be answered by historical replay.

And:

> What is the best explanation now?

can be answered by retrospective reassessment.

These are different.

---

# 462.57 Diagnostic contamination

A dangerous failure occurs when current evidence is silently inserted into historical diagnosis.

Example:

At 10:00:

$$
H_1
$$

was the best available explanation.

At 14:00:

new evidence proves:

$$
H_2.
$$

Historical replay at 10:00 must not use the 14:00 evidence.

Therefore:

$$
\boxed{
FutureEvidence\notin HistoricalDiagnosis.
}
$$

This extends the Future Evidence Non-Contamination principle.

---

# 462.58 Diagnostic completeness

Can we guarantee that all possible root causes are generated?

No.

Because:

$$
H_Q
$$

may itself be incomplete.

Therefore:

$$
DiagnosisComplete
$$

requires an explicit hypothesis-universe contract.

This is consistent with Step 378.

---

# 462.59 Unknown root cause

A legitimate diagnostic result is:

$$
RootCause=Unknown.
$$

This does not mean:

$$
NoCause.
$$

It means:

$$
AvailableEvidence
$$

does not establish a sufficiently supported cause.

Thus:

$$
UnknownCause\neq NoCause.
$$

---

# 462.60 Multiple root causes

Complex failures may genuinely have:

$$
H_1\land H_2\land H_3.
$$

Example:

```text id="n5k2zq"
High workload
+
insufficient capacity
+
poor timeout configuration
→
service failure
```

There may be no single root cause.

Therefore:

$$
\boxed{
RootCause\ need\ not\ be\ singular.
}
$$

This is a major architectural implication.

---

# 462.61 Root-cause selection is normative

Even after identifying causal factors, deciding which one to call "the root cause" can depend on purpose.

Operations may choose:

> configuration defect.

Security may choose:

> unauthorized change.

Architecture may choose:

> inadequate capacity design.

Governance may choose:

> missing change control.

All can describe different projections of the same history.

Thus:

$$
RootCause_{Ops}
\neq
RootCause_{Security}
\neq
RootCause_{Governance}
$$

without contradiction.

---

# 462.62 Root cause as projection

This gives a powerful KnowledgeOS interpretation:

$$
\boxed{
RootCause=Projection_\Gamma(H,E,Q).
}
$$

There is no universal root-cause object.

---


These are application/domain constructs.

# 462.64 Diagnostic bounded context

A useful optional bounded context is:

```text id="4r6m0o"
DIAGNOSTIC INTELLIGENCE
```

Responsibilities:

* detect diagnostic situations;
* generate hypotheses;
* retrieve evidence;
* assess evidence;
* construct fault/event trees;
* perform causal analysis;
* maintain competing explanations;
* perform counterfactual analysis;
* identify information gaps;
* recommend diagnostic tests;
* preserve diagnostic provenance.

It does not own:

* truth;
* universal causality;
* authority;
* final decision.

# 462.66 Normal-PC implementation

# 462.72 Diagnostic uncertainty

### Definition

**Diagnostic uncertainty** is unresolved uncertainty among competing explanations of an observed phenomenon.

It may result from:

* missing observations;
* ambiguous evidence;
* dependent sources;
* insufficient discrimination;
* competing causal models;
* incomplete hypothesis space.

Thus:

$$
DiagnosticUncertainty
$$

is broader than probability.

# 462.73 Epistemic versus aleatoric diagnostic uncertainty

### Epistemic

We lack sufficient knowledge to identify the cause.

### Aleatoric

The phenomenon itself may have multiple stochastic causes.

Example:

A random hardware failure may be intrinsically variable.

But not knowing which component failed is epistemic.

The distinction remains regime-dependent.
