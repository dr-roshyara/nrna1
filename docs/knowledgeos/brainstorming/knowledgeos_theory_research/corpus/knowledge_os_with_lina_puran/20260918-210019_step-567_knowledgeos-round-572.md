# KnowledgeOS — Round 572

## Unified Stopping Theory

I have continued from Round 571 and re-read the relevant roadmap section. The attached roadmap explicitly identifies this as **TODO #13 — Unified stopping theory** and asks us to reconcile:

$$
Stop\iff Suf_{Det}\land Suf_{Stab}\land Suf_{Evidence}\land GovernancePermits
$$

with the broader:

$$
PolicyGate=
TargetAdequacy\land ModelAdequacy\land ActionFeasibility
\land EvidenceAdequacy\land GovernancePermission.
$$

The stated research question is:

$$
\boxed{\text{When may KnowledgeOS legitimately transition from inquiry to determination/action?}}
$$



I think this round produces an important architectural correction:

$$
\boxed{
\textbf{There should not be one universal STOP predicate.}
}
$$

There are at least **two different stopping decisions**:

$$
\boxed{StopInquiry}
$$

and

$$
\boxed{PermitAction}.
$$

They must not be collapsed.

---

# 1. Why the existing STOP formula is too compressed

The earlier formula was:

$$
Stop
\iff
Suf_{Det}
\land
Suf_{Stab}
\land
Suf_{Evidence}
\land
GovernancePermits.
$$

The problem is that "stop" is ambiguous.

Does it mean:

1. stop collecting evidence?
2. stop semantic investigation?
3. stop model investigation?
4. establish a determination?
5. make a decision?
6. execute an action?

These are not the same event.

For example:

> We know with sufficient certainty that a voter is eligible.

does not imply:

> The system is authorized to cast a vote on behalf of the voter.

Therefore:

$$
\boxed{
StoppingInquiry\neq AuthorizingAction.
}
$$

---

# 2. Define stopping

A **Stopping Decision** is a determination that no further inquiry is required for a specified target under a specified contract and scope.

Formally:

$$
Stop_I(E,Q,C,t)
$$

means:

> continuing inquiry is not required to satisfy the declared inquiry target under \(Q,C,t\).

This is **not** a statement that absolute truth has been obtained.

---

# 3. Define action permission separately

An **Action Permission** is a governance determination that a specified action may be executed under the applicable authorization rules.

$$
Permit_A(E,D,A,C,G,t)
$$

means:

> the action \(A\), based on determination \(D\), satisfies the applicable governance conditions.

Therefore:

$$
Stop_I\not\Rightarrow Permit_A.
$$

This is one of the most important results of this round.

---

# 4. The distinction in a real-world example

Suppose KnowledgeOS determines:

$$
Eligible(VoterX)=True.
$$

Inquiry:

> Is Voter X eligible?

The inquiry can stop.

But another question remains:

> May the system automatically cast a vote?

Possibly not.

So:

$$
Stop_I=True
$$

while:

$$
Permit_A=False.
$$

This is not a contradiction.

It is exactly what a governance-aware epistemic system should permit.

---

# 5. Define inquiry target

An **Inquiry Target** is the specific property, relation, determination, or decision-relevant object that the inquiry seeks to establish.

Examples:

$$
Z_1=Eligible(X)
$$

$$
Z_2=Winner(Election)
$$

$$
Z_3=ModelAdequate(M)
$$

$$
Z_4=ActionFeasible(A).
$$

Without a target, "sufficient knowledge" has no precise meaning.

Therefore:

$$
\boxed{
Sufficiency\ is\ always\ relative\ to\ a\ target.
}
$$

---

# 6. Define stopping contract

A **Stopping Contract** specifies what must be satisfied before inquiry may terminate.

I recommend:

$$
\boxed{
SC=
(Target,
Scope,
RequiredConditions,
Tolerance,
StabilityScope,
EvidenceRules,
Time,
AcquisitionPolicy,
GovernanceRelation,
Version)
}
$$

This is a contract/value object in L1.

---

# 7. The first major correction

We should replace:

$$
Stop
$$

with:

$$
\boxed{
Stop_I(Q,Z,C,\Gamma,t)
}
$$

where:

* \(Q\) = inquiry;
* \(Z\) = target;
* \(C\) = contract;
* \(\Gamma\) = applicable regime;
* \(t\) = relevant time.

This makes stopping explicitly **inquiry-relative**.

---

# 8. Determination stopping condition

From Round 571, define:

$$
DetReady
$$

as:

$$
\begin{aligned}
DetReady\iff&
TargetAdequate\\
&\land SemanticAdequate\\
&\land EvidenceAdequate\\
&\land ModelAdequate\\
&\land LogicalValid\\
&\land ApproximationSafe\\
&\land LifecycleValid\\
&\land DeterminationSufficient\\
&\land DeterminationStable\\
&\land NoMaterialUnresolvedUncertainty.
\end{aligned}
$$

This is already substantially stronger than the old stopping formula.

---

# 9. But there is another problem

Some conditions are irrelevant for a particular inquiry.

For example, model adequacy is meaningless if no model is used.

Therefore:

$$
ModelAdequacy=NotApplicable
$$

must be allowed.

Likewise:

$$
ApproximationSafety=NotApplicable.
$$

Hence:

$$
\boxed{
NotApplicable\neq Failed.
}
$$

This continues the four-valued/typed logic we established earlier.

---

# 10. Condition algebra

Each stopping condition should have:

$$
CS\in
\{
Satisfied,
Failed,
Unknown,
Undefined,
Conditional,
NotApplicable
\}.
$$

This gives us six semantically different states.

For example:

| Condition              | Status        |
| ---------------------- | ------------- |
| SemanticAdequacy       | Satisfied     |
| EvidenceAdequacy       | Satisfied     |
| ModelAdequacy          | NotApplicable |
| LifecycleValidity      | Satisfied     |
| DeterminationStability | Unknown       |
| GovernancePermission   | Satisfied     |

The system must not turn this into a scalar confidence value.

---

# 11. Define stopping readiness

Let:

$$
SR(Q,Z,E,C)
$$

be the stopping-readiness assessment.

It should return:

$$
\boxed{
\{
Ready,
Blocked,
ConditionallyReady,
Undefined
\}
}
$$

with explicit reasons.

---

# 12. Why “confidence > 95%” is inadequate

Consider two situations.

### Situation A

$$
Confidence=0.97
$$

but:

$$
SemanticAdequacy=Unknown.
$$

### Situation B

$$
Confidence=0.90
$$

but all semantic, logical, evidence and lifecycle conditions are formally satisfied and the remaining uncertainty is immaterial to the target.

A scalar confidence threshold cannot distinguish the nature of these situations.

KnowledgeOS therefore should not define:

$$
Stop\iff Confidence>\theta.
$$

That would collapse fundamentally different epistemic states.

---

# 13. Materiality becomes central

From Round 570:

$$
Material(U_i,Z)
$$

means that variation in \(U_i\) can change target \(Z\).

Therefore define:

$$
NoMaterialUncertainty(Z)
$$

as:

$$
\forall U_i:
Unresolved(U_i)
\Rightarrow
\neg Material(U_i,Z).
$$

This is one of the strongest candidates for the stopping calculus.

---

# 14. Stopping does not require zero uncertainty

This is now formally clear.

Suppose:

$$
U_{model}>0.
$$

But:

$$
Det(M_1)=Det(M_2)=Det(M_3)=A.
$$

Then:

$$
DS=True
$$

and:

$$
Material(U_{model},Z)=False.
$$

Therefore inquiry can potentially stop.

Thus:

$$
\boxed{
Stop\neq Uncertainty=0.
}
$$

---

# 15. Finite exhaustive test

I constructed a synthetic state space with:

$$
2^8=256
$$

epistemic/governance states.

The dimensions were:

$$
(S,E,M,L,St,G,A,U)
$$

where:

* \(S\) = semantic adequacy;
* \(E\) = evidence adequacy;
* \(M\) = model adequacy;
* \(L\) = lifecycle validity;
* \(St\) = stability;
* \(G\) = governance permission;
* \(A\) = action feasibility;
* \(U\) = material unresolved uncertainty.

The target determination was deliberately fixed to \(A\), allowing us to isolate the stopping semantics.

This is **synthetic exhaustive computation**, not empirical evidence.

---

# 16. Result 1 — same epistemic situation, different governance

Consider:

$$
(S,E,M,L,St,U)
=
(1,1,1,1,1,0).
$$

In state 1:

$$
G=0,\quad A=0.
$$

In state 2:

$$
G=1,\quad A=1.
$$

The epistemic conditions are identical.

Both satisfy:

$$
Stop_I=True.
$$

But:

$$
Permit_A(state_1)=False
$$

and:

$$
Permit_A(state_2)=True.
$$

Therefore:

$$
\boxed{
\textbf{Identical epistemic readiness can coexist with different action permissions.}
}
$$

This computationally proves the need to separate:

$$
Stop_I
$$

from:

$$
Permit_A.
$$

---

# 17. Result 2 — different epistemic states can yield the same determination

I also introduced an irrelevant hidden state \(H\).

Two worlds:

$$
H_1=(0,S=1,E=1,M=1,L=1,St=1,G=1,A=1,U=0)
$$

and:

$$
H_2=(1,S=1,E=1,M=1,L=1,St=1,G=1,A=1,U=0).
$$

They differ in the hidden world state:

$$
H_1\neq H_2.
$$

Yet the inquiry target is identical:

$$
Z(H_1)=Z(H_2)=A.
$$

Both satisfy the stopping conditions.

Therefore:

$$
\boxed{
\text{Complete world equality is unnecessary for stopping.}
}
$$

This is an important computational confirmation of our central principle:

$$
\boxed{
KnowledgeOS\ seeks\ target\ sufficiency,
not\ world\ reconstruction.
}
$$

---

# 18. The quotient interpretation

This connects beautifully to Round 565/568.

Define:

$$
H_1\sim_Z H_2
$$

iff:

$$
Z(H_1)=Z(H_2).
$$

Then stopping does not require identifying the exact \(H\).

It requires identifying the correct equivalence class with respect to \(Z\).

So:

$$
\boxed{
KnowledgeOS\ may\ stop\ when\ the\ remaining\ admissible\ states\ lie\ within\ one\ target\text{-}equivalence\ class.
}
$$

That is a much more fundamental statement than "confidence is high."

---

# 19. Determination sufficiency as quotient identification

Recall:

$$
\mathcal D(D)=
\{Det(H):H\in\mathcal H(D)\}.
$$

Then:

$$
DS(D)\iff|\mathcal D(D)|=1.
$$

Equivalently:

$$
\boxed{
\forall H_1,H_2\in\mathcal H(D):
Det(H_1)=Det(H_2).
}
$$

So all remaining epistemic alternatives belong to the same determination equivalence class.

This gives us a clean mathematical interpretation of determination sufficiency.

---

# 20. Stability is different

Suppose:

$$
Det(H)=A
$$

for all current admissible states.

But a permitted perturbation of the evidence/model/context changes it to:

$$
B.
$$

Then:

$$
DS=True
$$

but:

$$
S_{Det}=False.
$$

Thus:

$$
\boxed{
DeterminationSufficiency\neq DeterminationStability.
}
$$

Both are required when the stopping contract demands stability.

---

# 21. Stability scope matters

We must specify what perturbations are considered.

Let:

$$
\Sigma
$$

be the admissible perturbation set.

Then:

$$
Stable_Z
\iff
\forall s_1,s_2\in\Sigma:
Z(s_1)=Z(s_2).
$$

But if \(\Sigma\) changes, stability can change.

Therefore:

$$
\boxed{
Stability\ is\ contract\text{-}relative.
}
$$

---

# 22. A crucial distinction: stopping inquiry vs stopping forever

Suppose:

$$
Stop_I(t)=True.
$$

That does **not** mean:

$$
\forall t'>t,\ Stop_I(t')=True.
$$

A new event can arrive.

For example:

```text id="l6q1yn"
09:00  Evidence sufficient
09:05  Determination established
10:00  New evidence
10:01  Previous evidence retracted
10:02  Determination revised
```

Therefore stopping is a **time-indexed state**, not a permanent declaration.

---

# 23. Lifecycle integration

We should therefore define:

$$
Stop_I(t)
$$

as a current property of the reconstructed epistemic state:

$$
H_{0:t}.
$$

When a new event arrives:

$$
H_{0:t+1}=H_{0:t}\cup\{e_{t+1}\},
$$

the stopping predicate is recalculated.

This is fully consistent with Round 569.

---

# 24. Event-sourced stopping

Therefore:

$$
\boxed{
Stop_I(t)
=
EvaluateStop(
Fold(H_{0:t}),
Q,C,\Gamma,t
)
}
$$

rather than storing:

```text
stopped = true
```

as an irreversible fact.

A stored "stopped" value can be a cache/read model, but the authoritative state must be reconstructible.

---

# 25. Governance integration

Now consider:

$$
Stop_I=True.
$$

This only means:

> no further inquiry is required for the declared target.

It does not mean:

$$
ActionAuthorized=True.
$$

Thus the pipeline becomes:

$$
\boxed{
Inquiry
\rightarrow
Determination
\rightarrow
StopInquiry
\rightarrow
Decision
\rightarrow
Authorization
\rightarrow
Action.
}
$$

This is cleaner than the previous combined model.

---

# 26. Action feasibility

The roadmap's `ActionFeasibility` belongs to the action layer, not necessarily the epistemic stopping layer.

For action \(a\):

$$
AF(a,E,C)
$$

means:

> action \(a\) is executable under the relevant technical, operational and policy constraints.

Example:

$$
Decision=SendNotification
$$

but:

$$
ActionFeasibility=False
$$

because the required communication channel is unavailable.

The inquiry itself may still have legitimately stopped.

---

# 29. Stopping as a decision problem

Now we encounter an important issue.

Suppose the inquiry is:

> Is more evidence worth acquiring?

Stopping itself is an **acquisition decision**.

Therefore:

$$
Stop
$$

cannot be determined only by epistemic sufficiency.

There may be cases where:

$$
DS=True
$$

but additional information has significant decision value because the target is not the determination itself but a downstream decision.

This connects to our previous:

$$
MVoI,\ DG,\ SG,\ VoI.
$$

# 30. Determination sufficiency versus planning sufficiency

We previously demonstrated:

$$
\boxed{
DeterminationSufficiency\neq PlanningSufficiency.
}
$$

This means:

$$
DS=True
$$

does not automatically imply:

$$
StopAcquisition=True.
$$

The inquiry target matters.

# 31. Example

Suppose current determination is:

$$
Eligible=True.

That may be sufficient for the eligibility inquiry.

But if the next decision is:

So:

$$
Stop_I(Eligible)=True
$$

while:

$$
Stop_{Planning}=False.
$$

This is not inconsistency.

They have different targets.

# 34. Acquisition should stop when marginal value is insufficient

Under a probabilistic decision-theoretic regime, a candidate rule could be:

$$
\boxed{
Stop_{Acq}
\iff
\max_a VoI(a)
\leq Cost(a)
}
$$

but **only under that declared utility/probability regime**.

This must not become a universal KnowledgeOS theorem.

In another regime, acquisition may be required because of:

* legal obligation;
* governance rule;
* audit requirement;
* safety requirement;
* minimum evidence requirement.

Therefore:

$$
\boxed{
Economic\ stopping\neq Epistemic\ stopping\neq Governance\ stopping.
}
$$

# 35. Three stopping concepts

This suggests three explicit concepts:

### Epistemic stopping

$$
Stop_E
$$

No material epistemic uncertainty remains for the target.

### Decision-theoretic stopping

$$
Stop_V
$$

No further acquisition has sufficient value under the declared value model.

### Governance stopping

$$
Stop_G
$$

The applicable governance contract permits inquiry termination.

The actual system may require:

$$
\boxed{
Stop=
Stop_E
\land
Stop_G
\land
Stop_V
}
$$

when all three are required.

But not universally.

# 36. This avoids a hidden mistake

Previously we could accidentally assume:

$$
Stop_E\Rightarrow Stop_V.
$$

That is false.

And:

$$
Stop_V\Rightarrow Stop_G
$$

is also false.

For example, an economically optimal action may still be prohibited by governance.

# 40. Counterexample-driven stopping validation

For any proposed stopping rule \(R\), we should search for:

$$
H_1,H_2
$$

such that:

$$
R(H_1)=Stop
$$

but:

$$
Z(H_1)\neq Z(H_2)
$$

for another admissible state \(H_2\).

If found, then the stopping rule is unsound.

This gives us a practical automated test.

---

# 41. Formal stopping soundness criterion

Let:

$$
\mathcal H_C(E)
$$

be the admissible states under contract \(C\).

A stopping rule is **target-sound** if:

$$
Stop_Z(E)
\Rightarrow
\forall H_1,H_2\in\mathcal H_C(E):
Z(H_1)=Z(H_2).
$$

Therefore:

$$
\boxed{
Stop_Z\Rightarrow DS_Z.
}
$$

This is a necessary condition for epistemically sound stopping.

---

# 42. But DS is not sufficient

The converse does not necessarily hold:

$$
DS_Z\not\Rightarrow Stop_Z.
$$

Why?

Because:

* evidence may not meet the required standard;
* model may be unvalidated;
* lifecycle may be invalid;
* stability may be required but absent;
* governance may require additional evidence;
* action context may require more information.

Thus:

$$
\boxed{
Stop_Z\subseteq DS_Z
}
$$

under contracts where stopping has stricter requirements.

---

# 43. This gives us a beautiful logical structure

$$
\boxed{
Stop_Z
\Rightarrow
LegitimateDet_Z
\Rightarrow
DS_Z
}
$$

under the soundness contract.

But:

$$
DS_Z
\not\Rightarrow
LegitimateDet_Z
$$

and:

$$
LegitimateDet_Z
\not\Rightarrow
PermitAction.
$$

This is one of the cleanest logical chains yet in KnowledgeOS.
