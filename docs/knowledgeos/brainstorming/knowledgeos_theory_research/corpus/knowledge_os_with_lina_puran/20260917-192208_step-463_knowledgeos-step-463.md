# KnowledgeOS — Step 463

## Hypothesis Prioritization, Diagnostic Search, Resource-Bounded Reasoning, Active Diagnosis, Experiment Design, Exploration–Exploitation, Information Value, Cost-Sensitive Diagnosis and “What Should We Investigate Next?”

Step 462 established that an observed problem can have several competing explanations:

$$
O\rightarrow\{H_1,H_2,\ldots,H_n\}
$$

and that:

$$
MostLikely\neq Determined.
$$

The next problem is operationally crucial.

If there are 100 or 10,000 plausible hypotheses, KnowledgeOS cannot investigate all of them equally.

Therefore:

$$
\boxed{
\text{KnowledgeOS must be able to choose what to investigate next without confusing priority with truth.}
}
$$

The central question of Step 463 is:

$$
\boxed{
\text{Can “next best investigation” be reduced to existing KnowledgeOS structures?}
}
$$

My preliminary conclusion is:

$$
\boxed{\textbf{YES — no new Kernel primitive is required.}}
$$

But this step reveals a potentially important **Epistemic Search / Active Diagnosis capability** at L3.

---

# 463.1 Resource-bounded reasoning

### Definition

**Resource-bounded reasoning** means reasoning under finite constraints such as:

* time;
* computation;
* money;
* human attention;
* available experiments;
* data access;
* operational risk.

An ideal reasoner might evaluate every hypothesis.

A real system cannot.

Therefore:

$$
Reasoning_\Gamma(H,R)
$$

must account for resource set \(R\).

---

# 463.2 Why resource bounds matter

Suppose:

$$
H=\{H_1,\ldots,H_{1000}\}.
$$

There are potentially 1,000 explanations.

But we have:

$$
30\text{ minutes}.
$$

We cannot perform 1,000 investigations.

Therefore KnowledgeOS needs a search strategy.

But:

$$
Priority(H_i)\neq Truth(H_i).
$$

A high-priority hypothesis is simply one that deserves investigation sooner.

---

# 463.3 Hypothesis prioritization

### Definition

**Hypothesis prioritization** is ordering candidate hypotheses according to an explicit criterion for investigation.

$$
Priority_\Gamma(H_i)
$$

may depend on:

* probability;
* impact;
* urgency;
* discriminability;
* investigation cost;
* safety;
* reversibility;
* decision relevance.

A useful conceptual form is:

$$
Priority(H_i)=
f(
P(H_i),
Impact(H_i),
Urgency(H_i),
Cost(H_i),
Discrimination(H_i)
).
$$

But there is no universal \(f\).

---

# 463.4 Priority is not probability

Suppose:

$$
P(H_1)=0.7
$$

and:

$$
P(H_2)=0.1.
$$

If \(H_2\) represents catastrophic system failure and is extremely cheap to test, it may deserve investigation first.

Thus:

$$
\boxed{
Priority\neq Probability.
}
$$

---

# 463.5 Priority is not truth

A hypothesis may be prioritized because it is:

* cheap to test;
* dangerous if missed;
* highly discriminative.

It does not become more true merely because it is investigated first.

$$
Priority(H)\not\Rightarrow Truth(H).
$$

---

# 463.6 Diagnostic search

### Definition

**Diagnostic search** is the process of exploring candidate explanations and evidence to reduce diagnostic uncertainty.

Conceptually:

$$
Search(H,E,Q,\Gamma)\rightarrow A.
$$

The search is adaptive because each observation can change what should be investigated next.

---

# 463.7 Search tree

A **search tree** represents possible investigative sequences.

Example:

```text id="8dbw6c"
Observed latency increase
        │
        ├── Test network
        │      ├── normal
        │      └── abnormal
        │
        ├── Test database
        │      ├── normal
        │      └── abnormal
        │
        └── Test configuration
               ├── unchanged
               └── changed
```

Each test creates branches.

The tree is a computational representation, not a new ontology primitive.

---

# 463.8 Search space

### Definition

A **search space** is the set of candidate states, hypotheses, actions or investigative paths considered by a search algorithm.

$$
\mathcal S_\Gamma.
$$

Important:

$$
SearchSpace\neq RealitySpace.
$$

The search space can be incomplete.

This repeats the earlier hypothesis-space result.

---

# 463.9 Search strategy

### Definition

A **search strategy** determines how candidates are explored.

Examples:

* breadth-first search;
* depth-first search;
* best-first search;
* A*;
* beam search;
* Monte Carlo Tree Search;
* Bayesian optimization;
* active learning;
* greedy information gain.

These are computational regimes.

They are not KnowledgeOS primitives.

---

# 463.10 Greedy search

A **greedy strategy** selects the locally best-looking next investigation.

For example:

$$
T^*=\arg\max_T Score(T).
$$

It is computationally cheap.

But:

$$
Greedy\neq GloballyOptimal.
$$

A test that looks best immediately may lead to a poor downstream investigation path.

---

# 463.11 Best-first search

**Best-first search** selects the candidate with the highest current evaluation.

$$
n^*=\arg\max_n Priority(n).
$$

This is useful for diagnostic investigation.

But again:

$$
Priority\neq Truth.
$$

---

# 463.12 A* reasoning

A* uses:

$$
f(n)=g(n)+h(n)
$$

where:

* \(g(n)\) = cost already incurred;
* \(h(n)\) = estimated remaining cost.

A* is useful when a meaningful state-space cost model exists.

But diagnostic reasoning often lacks a reliable universal heuristic.

Therefore A* is an optional computational regime.

---

# 463.13 Active diagnosis

### Definition

**Active diagnosis** means choosing observations/tests specifically to distinguish among competing diagnostic hypotheses.

This differs from passive diagnosis.

### Passive

Observe whatever happens.

### Active

Choose what to observe next.

Thus:

$$
\boxed{
ActiveDiagnosis=
Hypothesis\rightarrow TestSelection\rightarrow Observation.
}
$$

---

# 463.14 Diagnostic test

A **diagnostic test** is an observation-producing procedure intended to distinguish candidate hypotheses.

Let:

$$
T
$$

be a test.

Its result:

$$
Y_T.
$$

The test is valuable if different hypotheses predict different results.

---

# 463.15 Test discrimination

### Definition

**Test discrimination** is the degree to which a test can distinguish competing hypotheses.

For hypotheses \(H_1,H_2\):

$$
P(Y|H_1)
$$

versus:

$$
P(Y|H_2).
$$

If these distributions are nearly identical, the test has little discriminatory value.

If they differ strongly, the test is useful.

---

# 463.16 Perfect diagnostic test

An idealized test \(T\) perfectly distinguishes \(H_1\) and \(H_2\) if:

$$
P(Y=a|H_1)=1
$$

and:

$$
P(Y=a|H_2)=0.
$$

Real tests are rarely perfect.

---

# 463.17 Information gain

### Definition

**Information gain** measures reduction in uncertainty after obtaining an observation.

Under a probabilistic regime:

$$
IG(T)=
H(Hypotheses)
-
E_Y[H(Hypotheses|Y)].
$$

where \(H(\cdot)\) is entropy.

A high-information test sharply distinguishes possibilities.

But:

$$
\boxed{
InformationGain\neq KnowledgeGain.
}
$$

Already established.

---

# 463.18 Expected information gain

Before performing a test, we do not know its result.

Therefore:

$$
EIG(T)
=
E_Y[IG(T,Y)].
$$

This allows tests to be compared before execution.

---

# 463.19 Mutual information

A common measure is:

$$
I(H;Y)
=
\sum_{h,y}
P(h,y)
\log
\frac{P(h,y)}
{P(h)P(y)}.
$$

It measures statistical dependence between hypothesis \(H\) and test result \(Y\).

High mutual information means the test result tells us more about the hypothesis variable.

But:

$$
MutualInformation\neq CausalInformation.
$$

---

# 463.20 Value of information

### Definition

**Value of Information (VoI)** measures the expected improvement in decision quality from obtaining additional information.

A conceptual form is:

$$
VOI(T)
=
E_Y
[
\max_d EU(d|Y)
]
-
\max_d EU(d)
-
Cost(T).
$$

The exact formulation depends on the decision regime.

This is stronger than information gain because it considers the eventual decision.

---

# 463.21 Information gain versus decision value

Consider two tests.

### Test A

Very informative but changes no decision.

$$
IG(A)=high
$$

but:

$$
VOI(A)\approx0.
$$

### Test B

Only slightly reduces uncertainty but determines whether a €1 million action is authorized.

$$
IG(B)=moderate
$$

but:

$$
VOI(B)=high.
$$

Therefore:

$$
\boxed{
InformationValue\neq DecisionValue.
}
$$

---

# 463.22 Epistemic value

### Definition

**Epistemic value** is the value of information for improving understanding, discrimination, determination or epistemic state under an explicit epistemic objective.

It may differ from decision utility.

$$
EpistemicValue\neq DecisionUtility.
$$

A scientific experiment may have enormous epistemic value even if it has little immediate economic value.

---

# 463.23 Query value

### Definition

**Query value** is the expected benefit of obtaining an answer to a particular query.

For example:

> Is the Cloud First policy actually mandatory?

This may be extremely valuable in the Nexus decision.

---

# 463.24 Diagnostic value

### Definition

**Diagnostic value** is the expected usefulness of an investigation for distinguishing competing explanations and improving subsequent diagnosis or decision-making.

Candidate:

$$
DV(T)
=
f(
Discrimination,
DecisionImpact,
Cost,
Risk
).
$$

Again, this is a regime-specific construction.

---

# 463.25 Cost-sensitive diagnosis

### Definition

**Cost-sensitive diagnosis** explicitly considers the cost of diagnostic actions.

Costs may include:

* money;
* time;
* CPU/GPU;
* human effort;
* operational disruption;
* security exposure;
* opportunity cost.

Thus:

$$
T_1
$$

may be more informative than \(T_2\), but if:

$$
Cost(T_1)\gg Cost(T_2),
$$

\(T_2\) may be preferred.

---

# 463.26 Risk-sensitive investigation

A diagnostic action itself can create risk.

Example:

Testing whether production storage fails by intentionally filling it is obviously dangerous.

Therefore:

$$
TestValue
$$

must be evaluated together with:

$$
TestRisk.
$$

A useful admissibility condition is:

$$
Safe(T)\land Feasible(T).
$$

Only then should value be considered.

This preserves the earlier hierarchy:

$$
Valid
\rightarrow
Authorized
\rightarrow
Safe
\rightarrow
Feasible
\rightarrow
Useful.
$$

---

# 463.27 Investigation budget

### Definition

An **investigation budget** is the maximum available resource for diagnostic investigation.

For example:

$$
Budget=4\text{ hours}.
$$

or:

$$
Budget=€5,000.
$$

or:

$$
Budget=10\text{ CPU-hours}.
$$

---

# 463.28 Attention budget

### Definition

An **attention budget** is the limited amount of human or computational attention available for reviewing candidates.

This is important for KnowledgeOS because a system that produces:

$$
10,000
$$

warnings may be less useful than one producing:

$$
20
$$

high-value warnings.

---

# 463.29 Triage

### Definition

**Triage** is prioritizing cases according to urgency, risk, impact or expected value.

Example:

```text id="8f5a7q"
100 anomalies
   ↓
20 potentially significant
   ↓
5 decision-critical
   ↓
2 immediate investigations
```

Triage does not determine truth.

---

# 463.30 Screening

### Definition

**Screening** is an initial low-cost process used to identify candidates for deeper investigation.

Example:

$$
10,000\text{ records}
\rightarrow
500\text{ candidates}
\rightarrow
50\text{ validated cases}.
$$

Screening prioritizes recall and cost efficiency.

---

# 463.31 Screening versus determination

Screening may deliberately tolerate false positives.

Determination requires stronger evidence.

Thus:

$$
Screening\neq Determination.
$$

This is particularly important for ML.

---

# 463.32 Candidate recall

### Definition

**Candidate recall** is the proportion of relevant candidates successfully generated by a candidate-generation stage.

$$
Recall=
\frac{RelevantCandidatesRetrieved}
{AllRelevantCandidates}.
$$

Low recall is dangerous because the correct hypothesis may never enter the assessment stage.

Therefore:

$$
\boxed{
CandidateGeneration\ is\ an\ epistemic\ bottleneck.
}
$$

---

# 463.33 Candidate precision

### Definition

Candidate precision is:

$$
Precision=
\frac{RelevantCandidatesRetrieved}
{AllCandidatesRetrieved}.
$$

High precision reduces workload.

But excessively optimizing precision can destroy recall.

---

# 463.34 Recall–precision trade-off

A screening system may choose:

$$
HighRecall
$$

at the expense of:

$$
LowerPrecision.
$$

Then downstream KnowledgeOS assessment removes false candidates.

This suggests:

$$
\boxed{
ML\ should\ often\ optimize\ candidate\ recall,\ not\ final\ truth.
}
$$

---

# 463.35 Beam search

### Definition

**Beam search** retains only the top \(k\) candidates at each step.

If:

$$
k=5,
$$

only five branches survive.

This is computationally efficient.

But it creates a dangerous possibility:

$$
CorrectHypothesis
$$

may be pruned early.

Therefore KnowledgeOS should preserve:

$$
CandidatePruningReason.
$$

---

# 463.36 Hypothesis pruning

### Definition

**Hypothesis pruning** removes or deprioritizes hypotheses based on an explicit criterion.

Important distinction:

$$
Pruned\neq False.
$$

A hypothesis can be removed from active search because:

* insufficient current priority;
* cost too high;
* contradicted by strong evidence;
* outside scope;
* dominated by another hypothesis.

Historical preservation should retain the pruning reason.

---

# 463.37 Hypothesis expansion

New evidence may produce new hypotheses:

$$
H_{t+1}\not\subseteq H_t.
$$

Therefore diagnostic search must permit:

$$
Expand(H).
$$

This repeats Step 424.

---

# 463.38 Exploration

### Definition

**Exploration** means investigating relatively uncertain or less-tested possibilities to gain information.

Example:

Testing an uncommon failure mode.

---

# 463.39 Exploitation

### Definition

**Exploitation** means focusing resources on currently promising hypotheses or actions.

Example:

Testing the most likely database issue first.

---

# 463.40 Exploration–exploitation trade-off

Too much exploitation:

$$
\rightarrow
EpistemicLockIn.
$$

Too much exploration:

$$
\rightarrow
ResourceWaste.
$$

Therefore a diagnostic system needs a context-specific balance.

---

# 463.41 Multi-armed bandit analogy

A bandit problem has several actions ("arms") with uncertain rewards.

The system must learn which arm is valuable while spending limited trials.

Examples of algorithms:

* epsilon-greedy;
* UCB;
* Thompson sampling.

These are useful computational analogies.

But we should not promote "bandit" into KnowledgeOS ontology.

---

# 463.42 Diagnostic bandit

Suppose:

$$
T_1=\text{network test}
$$

$$
T_2=\text{database test}
$$

$$
T_3=\text{configuration test}.
$$

Each has:

* cost;
* expected discrimination;
* risk.

Repeated investigations can update estimates.

This can be treated as a bandit-like optimization problem.

But:

$$
DiagnosticSearch\neq BanditProblem
$$

universally.

---

# 463.43 Active learning

### Definition

**Active learning** is ML training where the learner selects which examples should be labeled or acquired.

Instead of:

$$
RandomSample\rightarrow Label,
$$

we have:

$$
ModelUncertainty
\rightarrow
SelectExample
\rightarrow
Label
\rightarrow
Retrain.
$$

KnowledgeOS can use the same structure for epistemic information acquisition.

---

# 463.44 Query-by-committee

Multiple models:

$$
M_1,\ldots,M_k
$$

make predictions.

Cases where they disagree are prioritized.

Example:

$$
M_1=fraud
$$

$$
M_2=legitimate
$$

$$
M_3=fraud.
$$

This case may have high acquisition value.

But:

$$
ModelDisagreement\neq TruthUncertainty
$$

automatically.

---

# 463.45 Expected model disagreement

Disagreement can be used as a candidate signal:

$$
D(x)=Var(
f_1(x),\ldots,f_k(x)
).
$$

High disagreement indicates an opportunity for investigation.

It does not prove that the models are wrong.

---

# 463.46 Uncertainty sampling

An active learner may choose:

$$
x^*
=
\arg\min_x
|P(Y=1|x)-0.5|.
$$

This targets uncertain examples.

But uncertainty sampling is not universally optimal.

The most uncertain case may have zero decision relevance.

---

# 463.47 Decision-aware information acquisition

KnowledgeOS should therefore prefer:

$$
\boxed{
DecisionAwareAcquisition
}
$$

rather than pure uncertainty reduction.

A candidate investigation should be evaluated by:

$$
\text{Does this information potentially change or strengthen the decision?}
$$

---

# 463.48 Diagnostic action selection

We can formulate:

$$
T^*
=
\arg\max_{T\in\mathcal T_{adm}}
\left[
VOI_\Gamma(T)
-
Cost_\Gamma(T)
-
Risk_\Gamma(T)
\right].
$$

subject to:

$$
Safe_\Gamma(T)
$$

and:

$$
Authorized_\Gamma(T).
$$

This is not a universal KnowledgeOS equation.

It is a **decision-theoretic regime**.

---

# 463.49 Why feasibility must come first

Suppose:

$$
VOI(T_1)=100
$$

but:

$$
Safe(T_1)=False.
$$

Then \(T_1\) must not be selected.

Likewise:

$$
Authorized(T_1)=False
$$

may make it inadmissible.

Therefore:

$$
\boxed{
Value\ optimization\ comes\ after\ admissibility.
}
$$

---

The optimized sequence is:

$$
\boxed{
Generate
\rightarrow
Screen
\rightarrow
Check\ Admissibility
\rightarrow
Estimate\ Value
\rightarrow
Select
\rightarrow
Observe
\rightarrow
Update
}
$$

More explicitly:

$$
H
\rightarrow
CandidateTests
\rightarrow
Feasibility
\rightarrow
Safety
\rightarrow
Authorization
\rightarrow
Cost/Risk
\rightarrow
Information/DecisionValue
\rightarrow

$$

This is consistent with Step 403.

# 463.51 Example: Nexus diagnosis

Observed:

$$
Latency=2s
$$

instead of:

$$
200ms.
$$

Hypotheses:

$$
H_1=Network
$$

$$
H_2=Database
$$

$$
H_3=Configuration
$$

$$
H_4=Hardware.
$$

Candidate tests:

| Test                 |     Cost |     Risk | Discrimination |
| -------------------- | -------: | -------: | -------------- |
| network-local test   |      low |      low | high           |

It should not replace hardware merely because hardware failure is possible.

# 463.52 Sequential diagnosis

After:

$$
T_1=configuration\ diff
$$

we learn:

$$
NoChange.
$$

Now the posterior hypothesis space may change.

The next test should be recalculated:

$$
T_2^*=Select(H_{t+1}).
$$

Therefore:

$$
\boxed{
DiagnosticSearch\ is\ adaptive.
}
$$

---

# 463.53 Myopic versus sequential optimization

A **myopic strategy** chooses the best immediate test.

A **sequential strategy** considers future tests and information pathways.

Example:

Test A may be only moderately informative now but unlock three highly valuable downstream tests.

Therefore:

$$
ImmediateValue\neq LongTermSearchValue.
$$

---

# 463.54 Value of experimentation

A diagnostic test can also be an experiment.

An **experiment** deliberately changes or controls a variable to distinguish hypotheses.

Example:

$$
ConfigurationA
$$

versus:

$$
ConfigurationB.
$$

The difference in outcome provides evidence.

But production experiments require:

* safety;
* authorization;
* reversibility;
* experimental design.

---

# 463.55 A/B test

An **A/B test** compares two variants:

$$
A
$$

and:

$$
B.
$$

Randomized assignment can support causal inference.

But:

$$
A/BTest\neq UniversalCausalProof.
$$

Validity depends on design and assumptions.

---

# 463.56 Randomization

### Definition

**Randomization** assigns experimental units to conditions using a random mechanism.

Its purpose is to reduce systematic confounding under appropriate conditions.

It is a statistical/causal method, not a Kernel primitive.

---

# 463.57 Sequential experiment design

KnowledgeOS can choose:

$$
Experiment_{t+1}
$$

based on:

$$
Evidence_{\le t}.
$$

This creates:

$$
Experiment
\rightarrow
Observation
\rightarrow
Evidence
\rightarrow
HypothesisUpdate
\rightarrow
NextExperiment.
$$

This is active epistemic learning.

# 463.58 Human attention allocation

Some investigations require experts.

Suppose:

$$
100
$$

cases require human review.

KnowledgeOS can prioritize:

$$
TopK
$$

using:

* decision impact;
* uncertainty;
* risk;
* novelty;
* contradiction;
* governance criticality.

The human then reviews the highest-value cases.

This is not replacing the human.

It is **attention allocation**.

---

# 463.59 Human-in-the-loop

### Definition

A **human-in-the-loop** architecture requires human participation in selected stages.

Possible points:

* hypothesis approval;
* evidence interpretation;
* high-risk test approval;
* governance interpretation;
* final decision;
* exceptional cases.

Human participation should be explicit, not assumed.

---

# 463.60 Human-on-the-loop

A **human-on-the-loop** architecture allows automation but maintains monitoring and intervention authority.

This can be useful for low-risk repeated processes.

Again, the required level is governance-dependent.

---

# 463.61 Automation boundary

KnowledgeOS should classify actions:

$$
AutomationLevel=
\{
Inform,
Recommend,
RequireReview,
RequireApproval,
Execute
\}.
$$

This is a governance projection.

It should depend on:

* risk;
* reversibility;
* authority;
* uncertainty.

---

# 463.62 Diagnostic automation rule

A useful candidate principle:

$$
\boxed{
The\ higher\ the\ uncertainty,\ risk,\ irreversibility,\ or\ governance\ criticality,
the\ stronger\ the\ human/authority\ gate.
}
$$

This is not a universal theorem.

It is a governance design hypothesis.

# 463.63 Computational budget allocation

The same framework applies to ML computation.

Suppose a normal PC has:

$$
8\text{ CPU cores}
$$

and limited memory.

KnowledgeOS may choose:

* cheap retrieval first;
* small model next;
* expensive LLM only for ambiguous cases.

Thus:

$$
CheapScreening
\rightarrow
ExpensiveAssessment
$$

is often better than:

$$
ExpensiveModel\ on\ Everything.
$$

---

# 463.64 Cascaded intelligence

A practical architecture:

```text id="4f0bq7"
Level 1 — deterministic filters
          ↓
Level 2 — lexical retrieval
          ↓
Level 3 — embeddings
          ↓
Level 4 — small ML model
          ↓
Level 5 — local LLM
          ↓
Level 6 — statistical/causal analysis
          ↓
Level 7 — human/authority review
```

This creates computational efficiency while preserving epistemic control.

---

# 463.65 Why this matters for a normal PC

A normal PC does not need to run a huge model on every record.

KnowledgeOS can use:

$$
\boxed{
Progressive\ computational\ escalation.
}
$$

Most cases are handled cheaply.

Only high-value/ambiguous cases receive expensive computation.

This is probably one of the most important practical design principles for the user's normal-PC objective.

# 463.68 Epistemic search state

A useful application-level projection is:

$$
ESS_t=
(
H_t,
E_t,
B_t,
R_t,
T_t
)
$$

where:

* \(H_t\) = candidate hypotheses;
* \(E_t\) = available evidence;
* \(B_t\) = remaining budget;
* \(R_t\) = unresolved requirements;
* \(T_t\) = available tests/actions.

This is a **search state**, not a new ontological primitive.

---

# 463.69 Search transition

A diagnostic action:

$$
a_t
$$

produces:

$$
ESS_{t+1}
=
Update(ESS_t,a_t,o_t).
$$

This is simply the existing transition semantics.

Therefore:

$$
SearchTransition
\subseteq
T_\rho.
$$

Another successful reduction.

---

# 463.70 Search policy

A **search policy** maps the current search state to a next action:

$$
\pi(ESS_t)\rightarrow a_t.
$$

This is exactly the same mathematical structure used in reinforcement learning.

But KnowledgeOS should distinguish:

$$
DecisionPolicy
$$

from:

$$
SearchPolicy.
$$

The latter selects investigative actions, not organizational decisions.

---

# 463.71 Reinforcement learning

An RL system learns a policy:

$$
\pi(a|s)
$$

to maximize expected cumulative reward.

KnowledgeOS can use RL for:

* query ordering;
* diagnostic test selection;
* resource allocation;
* experiment scheduling.

But reward must be explicitly defined.

Therefore:

$$
RL\neq Knowledge.
$$

---

# 463.72 Reward hacking danger

If the search system is rewarded for:

$$
FastDiagnosis,
$$

it may learn to stop prematurely.

If rewarded for:

$$
HighConfidence,
$$

it may prefer unjustified certainty.

Therefore the objective must not be:

$$
MaximizeConfidence.
$$

Instead, it should include:

* correctness;
* calibration;
* abstention;
* evidence quality;
* decision outcomes;
* traceability.

---

# 463.73 A safer objective

A candidate objective is:

$$

# 463.74 Epistemic regret

### Definition

**Epistemic regret** is the loss incurred because the system acquired less useful information or selected a poorer investigation than another available strategy would have.

For strategy \(\pi\):

$$
Regret(\pi)
=
Value(\pi^*)-Value(\pi).
$$

This connects to Step 438's decision regret.

# 463.75 Diagnostic regret

A diagnostic strategy can regret:

* investigating the wrong hypothesis first;
* missing a critical hypothesis;
* spending too much resource;
* failing to acquire a decisive observation.

This should be evaluated retrospectively without contaminating the historical decision.

# 463.76 Search failure

A search may fail because:

1. hypothesis space incomplete;
2. candidate generation missed correct hypothesis;
3. evidence inaccessible;
4. tests unavailable;
5. budget exhausted;
6. hypotheses observationally indistinguishable;
7. causal model insufficient.

Therefore:

$$
SearchFailure\neq KnowledgeFailure.
$$

And:

$$
NoDetermination\neq NoCause.
$$

# 463.77 Diagnostic dead end

A **diagnostic dead end** occurs when available investigations no longer meaningfully discriminate among surviving hypotheses.

Example:

$$
H_1,H_2
$$

make identical predictions for all currently feasible tests.

Then:

$$
\forall T\in\mathcal T_{available},
\quad
P(Y_T|H_1)\approx P(Y_T|H_2).
$$

Further investigation may have little value.

---

# 463.78 Observational equivalence

Two hypotheses are **observationally equivalent** under an observation family if no permitted observation distinguishes them.

$$
H_1\equiv_{\mathcal O}H_2.
$$

Then the correct epistemic result may be:

$$
H_1\parallel H_2.
$$

No amount of repeated identical observations necessarily resolves the distinction.

This is an important stopping condition.

---

# 463.79 Decision equivalence

Two hypotheses can be epistemically different but decision-equivalent:

$$
H_1\neq H_2
$$

yet:

$$
Decision(H_1)=Decision(H_2).
$$

Then further investigation may have little decision value.

This is another reason:

$$
InformationGain\neq DecisionValue.
$$

---

# 463.80 Diagnostic stopping rule

We can therefore define an application-level stopping condition:

Stop investigating if:

$$
\boxed{
DecisionRobust
\lor
NoUsefulTest
\lor
VOI<Cost+Risk
\lor
BudgetExhausted
}
$$

under the selected regime.

This does not mean:

> the truth has been discovered.

It means:

> further investigation is not justified under the current objective and resource constraints.

That distinction is fundamental.
