# Step 552 — Determination-Preserving Active Acquisition

## 552.1 Research objective

We start from the refined state:

$$
D=(O,Q,\Gamma,E,C)
$$

and derive the currently admissible hidden hypotheses:

$$
\boxed{\mathcal H(D)}
$$

Then map every admissible hypothesis to the determination required by the inquiry:

$$
Det_{Q,\Gamma}:\mathcal H(D)\rightarrow\mathcal D
$$

and construct:

$$
\boxed{
\mathcal D(D)=
\{Det_{Q,\Gamma}(H)\mid H\in\mathcal H(D)\}
}
$$

This is the **Determination Image**.

The fundamental stopping criterion is:

$$
\boxed{
|\mathcal D(D)|=1
\Rightarrow
\text{current inquiry determination is invariant}
}
$$

This is more useful operationally than asking whether all hidden structure has been reconstructed.

# 552.9 Determination Gain

For a finite hypothesis space we can define a simple benchmark uncertainty:

$$
U_D(D)=|\mathcal D(D)|.
$$

For acquisition \(a\):

$$
\boxed{
DG(a)
=
U_D(D)
-
E_o[U_D(D\cup o_a)]
}
$$

where \(E_o\) is expectation over possible acquisition outcomes.

For an initial ambiguity:

$$
|\mathcal D(D)|=2
$$

and an action guarantees a singleton:

$$
E_o[|\mathcal D(D\cup o_a)|]=1.
$$

Therefore:

$$
DG(a)=2-1=1.
$$

This is a benchmark metric, **not a universal definition of epistemic value**.

The attached material correctly warns that entropy, classification error, ambiguity count, expected loss and regret may represent different utility contracts.

# 552.10 Information Gain

For comparison:

$$
IG(a)=H(\mathcal H(D))-E_o[H(\mathcal H(D|o_a))].
$$

This measures reduction of structural uncertainty.

It does **not** measure whether the inquiry's determination becomes resolved.

Therefore:

$$
IG>0
$$

does not imply:

$$
DG>0.
$$

This distinction is now experimentally demonstrable.

But I would **not** yet claim that determination-directed acquisition is superior in real-world systems. We have established the logic and demonstrated it in a finite synthetic environment.

---

# 552.32 The next step is now clearer

The next research question should no longer simply be:

> “Can ML choose a good acquisition?”

That is too early.

The stronger question is:

$$
\boxed{
\textbf{Step 553 — Can KnowledgeOS discover which acquisition actions are actually capable of separating determination classes?}
}
$$

That introduces **Acquisition Identifiability**.

Given:

$$
\mathcal H(D)
$$

partitioned into determination classes:

$$
\mathcal H(D)
=
H_A\cup H_B\cup\cdots
$$

we ask for each action:

$$
a:
\mathcal H(D)\rightarrow O_a.
$$

The key question becomes:

$$
\boxed{
\exists H_i,H_j:
Det(H_i)\neq Det(H_j)
\land
Obs(H_i,a)\neq Obs(H_j,a)?
}
$$

If yes, the action has **determination-separating capability**.

If no, then no amount of ML ranking can make that action useful for the current determination.

This leads to a very important hierarchy:

```text
Unknown
   ↓
Can the determination be separated?
   ↓
Can an available action separate it?
   ↓
Which actions can separate it?
   ↓
Which action has acceptable value/cost?
   ↓
Is acquisition authorized?
   ↓
Acquire
   ↓
Validate
   ↓
Update H(D)
   ↓
Recompute Determination Image
```

That is a much stronger foundation for KnowledgeOS than simply adding another ML model.

And it preserves the central principle reached in Steps 548–552:

$$
\boxed{
\textbf{KnowledgeOS does not optimize for maximum information.}
}
$$

It optimizes, subject to the inquiry and governance contracts, for:

$$
\boxed{
\textbf{the smallest epistemically sufficient state from which the required determination is invariant.}
}
$$
