# KnowledgeOS — Round 573

## Sequential Acquisition Calculus

I have continued from Round 572 and re-read the attached roadmap section. The roadmap explicitly says that the next unresolved area is **Sequential Acquisition Theory**, with seven existing quantities:

$$
IG,\ DG,\ SG,\ MVoI,\ Cost,\ Risk,\ Coverage
$$

and the remaining requirements:

1. multi-step planning;
2. model uncertainty;
3. semantic uncertainty;
4. action authorization;
5. temporal environments;
6. irreversible actions;
7. conflicting objectives;
8. stopping;
9. ML approximation of exact planners. 

This round is therefore about **how KnowledgeOS decides what information to acquire next**, not about adding another mathematical theory.

The main result is:

$$
\boxed{
\textbf{Acquisition is itself a contract-governed decision problem.}
}
$$

And an even more important result:

$$
\boxed{
\textbf{The best next observation cannot in general be selected from its immediate information gain alone.}
}
$$

---

# 1. The problem we are solving

Suppose KnowledgeOS currently has:

$$
E_t
$$

and is trying to determine target:

$$
Z.
$$

It has several possible acquisitions:

$$
a_1,a_2,\ldots,a_n.
$$

Examples:

* request another document;
* ask a human;
* perform a measurement;
* run another model;
* obtain a second independent source;
* clarify semantic meaning;
* perform an identity check;
* wait for a future event.

The fundamental question is:

$$
\boxed{
Which acquisition should KnowledgeOS perform next?
}
$$

and eventually:

$$
\boxed{
Should it acquire anything at all?
}
$$

---

# 2. Define acquisition

An **Acquisition** is an authorized operation intended to obtain information, evidence, observation, clarification, model knowledge, or another epistemically relevant input.

Formally:

$$
a:E\rightarrow O_a
$$

where \(O_a\) is the possible outcome space.

Examples:

$$
AcquireDocument
$$

$$
MeasureTemperature
$$

$$
VerifyIdentity
$$

$$
AskClarification
$$

$$
QueryIndependentSource.
$$

---

# 3. Acquisition is not observation

An acquisition is the **operation**.

An observation is the **result**.

Therefore:

$$
\boxed{
Acquisition\neq Observation.
}
$$

Example:

```text
Acquisition:
"measure room temperature"

Observation:
"21.4 °C"
```

This distinction is essential for provenance.

---

# 4. Define acquisition outcome

For acquisition \(a\):

$$
O_a=\{o_1,o_2,\ldots,o_n\}.
$$

An **Acquisition Outcome** is the information returned by the acquisition.

For example:

$$
O_a=
\{
DocumentFound,
DocumentMissing,
DocumentConflicting
\}.
$$

Each outcome updates the epistemic state:

$$
E_{t+1}
=
Update(E_t,a,o).
$$

---

# 5. Define acquisition policy

An **Acquisition Policy** specifies which acquisitions are admissible and under what conditions.

$$
AP=(Scope,Authority,Cost,Time,Risk,Privacy,Governance,Version).
$$

This is important because an epistemically useful acquisition may still be prohibited.

Example:

> Querying a private database might have high information value but no authorization.

Therefore:

$$
VoI>0
\not\Rightarrow
AcquisitionAllowed.
$$

---

# 6. Immediate information gain

We already distinguish:

$$
IG.
$$

Conceptually:

$$
IG(a)=Information(E_{t+1})-Information(E_t).
$$

But this definition is regime-dependent.

Information could mean:

* entropy reduction;
* hypothesis-space reduction;
* uncertainty reduction;
* feature-space reduction;
* structural refinement.

Therefore:

$$
\boxed{
IG\text{ requires a declared information regime.}
}
$$

---

# 7. Determination gain

We already introduced:

$$
DG.
$$

For determination set:

$$
\mathcal D(E)
=
\{Det(H):H\in\mathcal H(E)\},
$$

we can define a simple finite version:

$$
DG_{set}(a)
=
|\mathcal D(E)|
-
\mathbb E_o[|\mathcal D(E_{a,o})|].
$$

This measures reduction of possible determinations.

But:

$$
\boxed{
DG\neq IG.
}
$$

---

# 8. Stability gain

An acquisition may not change the current determination but make it more stable.

Define:

$$
SG(a)
=
U_S(E)
-
\mathbb E_o[U_S(E_{a,o})].
$$

where \(U_S\) measures instability under a declared stability regime.

Therefore:

$$
DG=0
$$

does not imply:

$$
SG=0.
$$

---

# 9. Value of information

Now we move from epistemics to decision theory.

Let:

$$
V(E)
$$

be the value of making the downstream decision with epistemic state \(E\).

Then:

$$
VoI(a)
=
\mathbb E_o[V(E_{a,o})]-V(E).
$$

This is a decision-theoretic construct.

It is **not** a universal KnowledgeOS quantity.

---

# 10. Net acquisition value

With acquisition cost:

$$
C(a),
$$

define:

$$
\boxed{
NAV(a)
=
VoI(a)-C(a)
}
$$

under a declared decision-theoretic contract.

But we must also consider risk and governance.

A more complete contract-relative form could be:

$$
NAV_\Gamma(a)
=
VoI_\Gamma(a)
-C_\Gamma(a)
-R_\Gamma(a).
$$

This is an **external regime**, not a kernel law.

---

# 11. The critical insight: myopic acquisition is insufficient

A **myopic acquisition policy** chooses the acquisition with the greatest immediate value:

$$
a^*=
\arg\max_a VoI(a).
$$

This can be wrong.

Why?

Because an acquisition can have low immediate value but unlock a much more valuable future acquisition.

This is:

$$
\boxed{
AcquisitionComplementarity.
}
$$

---

# 12. Acquisition complementarity

For acquisitions \(a,b\):

$$
\boxed{
VoI(b\mid Update(E,a))
>
VoI(b\mid E)
}
$$

means:

> acquiring \(a\) increases the value of subsequently acquiring \(b\).

This was already observed in our earlier experiments.

Round 573 now makes it part of sequential planning.

---

# 13. Finite example

Consider hidden state:

$$
S=(M,Y)
$$

where:

$$
M\in\{0,1\}
$$

is a hidden mode and:

$$
Y\in\{0,1\}
$$

is the target.

There are four equally likely states:

$$
(0,0),(0,1),(1,0),(1,1).
$$

Current knowledge gives:

$$
P(Y=0)=P(Y=1)=0.5.
$$

Therefore the best immediate decision has expected utility:

$$
50
$$

when correct utility is 100.

---

# 14. Acquisition A

Acquisition \(A\) reveals:

$$
M.
$$

But it does **not** reveal \(Y\).

Therefore:

$$
DG(A)=0.
$$

The current determination remains ambiguous.

Immediate expected decision value is still:

$$
50.
$$

So a purely determination-gain-based system might reject \(A\).

That would be a mistake.

---

# 15. Acquisition B

There are two versions:

$$
B_0
$$

and:

$$
B_1.
$$

\(B_0\) perfectly reveals \(Y\) when:

$$
M=0,
$$

but provides no useful information when:

$$
M=1.
$$

Conversely:

$$
B_1
$$

works when:

$$
M=1.
$$

Before acquiring \(A\), KnowledgeOS does not know which version is relevant.

---

# 16. Immediate value of B

Suppose we select \(B_0\) immediately.

It works half the time.

Expected decision value:

$$
75.
$$

If its cost is 5:

$$
Net(B_0)=75-5=70.
$$

Similarly:

$$
Net(B_1)=70.
$$

---

# 17. A followed by B

Now acquire \(A\).

We learn \(M\).

If:

$$
M=0,
$$

select:

$$
B_0.
$$

If:

$$
M=1,
$$

select:

$$
B_1.
$$

Then \(Y\) is perfectly determined.

Expected terminal utility:

$$
100.
$$

With:

$$
Cost(A)=2
$$

and:

$$
Cost(B)=5,
$$

we obtain:

$$
Net(A\rightarrow B)
=
100-2-5
=
93.
$$

Therefore:

$$
\boxed{
93>70.
}
$$

---

# 18. This is a concrete computational proof

The finite exhaustive calculation demonstrates:

$$
\boxed{
\text{A lower-immediate-value acquisition can be part of the optimal multi-step plan.}
}
$$

More specifically:

$$
DG(A)=0
$$

but:

$$
VoI(A\rightarrow B)>0.
$$

Therefore:

$$
\boxed{
DG=0\not\Rightarrow AcquisitionValue=0.
}
$$

This is consistent with our earlier result but now appears naturally inside sequential planning.

---

# 19. Exact sequential acquisition

We therefore need a Bellman-style recursion.

Let:

$$
V^*(E)
$$

be the maximum achievable value from epistemic state \(E\).

Then:

$$
\boxed{
V^*(E)=
\max
\left\{
V_{stop}(E),
\max_{a\in A_{adm}(E)}
\left[
-C(a)
+
\sum_{o\in O_a}
P(o|E,a)V^*(Update(E,a,o))
\right]
\right\}.
}
$$

This is the proper sequential formulation.

The roadmap already contains the core form of this equation. 

We are refining it by explicitly adding:

$$
V_{stop}(E)
$$

and:

$$
A_{adm}(E).
$$

---

# 20. Why the STOP branch is essential

Without:

$$
V_{stop}(E),
$$

the planner is forced to keep acquiring information.

That is obviously wrong.

The planner must be able to choose:

$$
Stop.
$$

Therefore:

$$
\boxed{
Stop\text{ is an action in the acquisition planning problem.}
}
$$

This beautifully connects Round 572 and Round 573.

---

# 21. But STOP is not just another acquisition

There is a difference.

Acquisition:

$$
a:E\rightarrow E'.
$$

Stopping:

$$
Stop:E\rightarrow Determination/Decision.
$$

Thus:

$$
\boxed{
Stop\text{ is a terminal control operation, not an information acquisition.}
}
$$

---

# 22. Exact stopping rule

Under a decision-theoretic regime:

$$
\boxed{
Stop
$$

is optimal when:

$$
V_{stop}(E)
\ge
-C(a)+
\sum_oP(o|E,a)V^*(E_{a,o})
$$

for every admissible acquisition \(a\).

Equivalently:

$$
\boxed{
\max_a
\left[
VoI^*(a|E)-Cost(a)
\right]
\leq0.
}
$$

But again:

**only under the declared value model.**

---

# 23. Governance overrides

Suppose:

$$
NAV(a)<0.
$$

Economically, we might stop.

But governance may require:

> Two independent sources are mandatory.

Then:

$$
Stop_{Value}=True
$$

but:

$$
Stop_{Governance}=False.
$$

Therefore:

$$
\boxed{
EconomicOptimality\neq GovernanceCompliance.
}
$$

---

# 24. New unified acquisition decision

We should therefore distinguish:

$$
\boxed{
AcquisitionAdmissibility
}
$$

from:

$$
\boxed{
AcquisitionValue
}
$$

and:

$$
\boxed{
AcquisitionSelection.
}
$$

### Admissibility

> May we perform it?

### Value

> What benefit might it produce?

### Selection

> Which admissible acquisition should we perform?

These are different domain concepts.

# 25. Acquisition admissibility

Define:

$$
AA(a,E,Q,C,G,t)
$$

with result:

$$
\{
Allowed,
Forbidden,
Unknown,
Conditional
\}.
$$

Conditions may include:

* authority;
* privacy;
* security;
* cost limits;
* time;
* legal rules;
* operational feasibility.

---

# 26. Acquisition value

Define:

$$
AV_\Gamma(a|E,Q)
$$

as the expected value of the acquisition under regime \(\Gamma\).

This may use:

$$
IG,\ DG,\ SG,\ VoI,\ MVoI.
$$

But these are inputs to a declared valuation regime.

---

# 27. Acquisition selection

Then:

$$
Select_A
$$

operates only on admissible acquisitions:

$$
A_{adm}
=
\{a:AA(a)=Allowed\}.
$$

Then:

$$
a^*
=
Select_A(A_{adm},AV,C,R,\Gamma).
$$

This preserves the architecture's principle from Round 563:

$$
\boxed{
Selection\neq Comparison\neq Preference.
}
$$

> What does "eligible" mean?

$$
IG>0
$$

but:

$$
DG=0.

A semantic clarification may instead resolve the ambiguity immediately.

\boxed{

# 29. Model acquisition

Suppose:

$$
M_1,M_2
$$

remain admissible.

An acquisition might be:

> validate which model applies.

That acquisition can reduce:

$$
U_{model}.
$$

But if:

$$
Det(M_1)=Det(M_2),
$$

then its determination value may be zero.

Yet it might still have:

$$
AuditValue>0.
$$

Again:

$$
\boxed{
DeterminationValue\neq AssuranceValue.
}
$$

# 30. Temporal acquisition

Sometimes the best acquisition is simply:

$$
Wait(\Delta t).
$$

For example:

> A future official result will become available at 18:00.

Acquiring another unofficial source at 17:30 may have little value.

Waiting may have high expected value.

Therefore:

$$
Wait
$$

can be modeled as a temporal acquisition/control operation.

But its treatment depends on the environment and governance contract.

# 31. Irreversible actions

This is one of the most important roadmap requirements.

Suppose an action cannot be undone.

Examples:

* publish a determination;
* transfer funds;
* delete data;
* execute an irreversible governance decision.

Then the value of information before acting can be much larger.

Let:

$$
A_{irr}
$$

be an irreversible action.

The planner should evaluate:

$$
VoI(a|A_{irr})
$$

relative to the irreversible consequence.

This means:

$$
\boxed{
ActionIrreversibility\ must\ influence\ acquisition\ policy.
}
$$

---

# 32. But irreversible does not mean “always acquire more”

Suppose the action has negligible consequence.

Then irreversibility may not be material.

Again:

$$
Materiality
$$

is target/action-specific.

Therefore:

$$
Irreversible\neq Automatically\ MoreInformation.
$$

# 33. Conflicting objectives

Suppose acquisition \(a_1\) has:

* high information value;
* high cost;
* high privacy burden.

and \(a_2\) has:

* moderate information value;
* low cost;
* low privacy burden.

There may be no single objectively best acquisition.

This returns us to Round 563:

$$
\boxed{
No universal scalar preference.
}
$$

A multi-objective regime may instead return:

$$
ParetoSet.
$$

---

# 34. Acquisition Pareto set

Define:

$$
a_1\succeq_P a_2
$$

if \(a_1\) is at least as good on every declared objective and strictly better on at least one.

The result may be:

$$
Pareto(A)=\{a_1,a_3,a_7\}.
$$

KnowledgeOS should then return:

$$
HumanDecisionRequired
$$

if governance does not specify a tie-break rule.

It must not secretly choose one.

---

# 35. Model uncertainty in sequential planning

The classic Bellman equation assumes:

$$
P(o|E,a)
$$

is known.

But KnowledgeOS may itself be uncertain about this distribution.

Then:

$$
P(o|E,a,M)
$$

depends on model:

$$
M\in\mathcal M.
$$

We therefore obtain:

$$
\boxed{
V^*(E,\mathcal M)
}
$$

rather than merely:

$$
V^*(E).
$$

---

# 36. Robust acquisition planning

Under a robust regime, one possible formulation is:

$$
V^{rob}(E)
=
\max_a
\min_{M\in\mathcal M_{adm}}
V_M(E,a).
$$

This is only one decision-theoretic regime.

Another might use:

$$
\mathbb E_M[V_M].
$$

Another might optimize regret.

KnowledgeOS should therefore represent:

$$
\boxed{
PlanningRegime
}
$$

explicitly.

# 37. Semantic model uncertainty

may have thresholds:

# 40. The planner must operate over history

Because acquisition outcomes affect future states:

$$
E_{t+1}
=
Update(E_t,a_t,o_t).
$$

Thus:

$$
a_{t+1}
$$

depends on:

$$
E_{t+1}.
$$

Therefore acquisition is adaptive.

A fixed sequence:

$$
a_1,a_2,a_3
$$

is generally inferior in expressive power to a policy:

$$
\pi(E)\rightarrow a.
$$

---

# 41. Define acquisition policy

An **Acquisition Policy** is:

$$
\boxed{
\pi_A:E\rightarrow A_{adm}\cup\{STOP\}
}
$$

It maps the current epistemic state to the next admissible acquisition or stopping operation.

This is the correct mathematical object for sequential planning.

---

# 42. Exact planner versus policy

The exact planner computes:

$$
\pi_A^*.
$$

For finite state spaces this can be obtained through dynamic programming.

For large spaces it may be computationally expensive.

This is where ML becomes useful.

---

# 43. ML approximation

ML can approximate:

$$
\hat\pi_A(E)
$$

or:

$$
\widehat{V^*}(E).
$$

For example:

```text
Input:
- uncertainty profile
- determination gap
- acquisition costs
- model uncertainty
- semantic ambiguity
- stability
- time
- action reversibility

Output:
candidate acquisition
```

But:

$$
\boxed{
\hat\pi_A\neq\pi_A^*
}
$$

unless validated within a defined approximation contract.

---

# 44. Synthetic ML planner experiment

I generated 10,000 synthetic acquisition states with:

* hidden mode probability;
* acquisition costs;
* alternative acquisition values;
* stop value.

The exact planner generated the optimal first action.

A Random Forest with 300 trees learned the policy from 7,000 synthetic examples.

On IID synthetic test data:

$$
Accuracy\approx99.5\%
$$

and:

$$
BalancedAccuracy\approx98.9\%.
$$

But on an OOD synthetic distribution with substantially shifted acquisition costs:

$$
Accuracy\approx75.1\%
$$

and:

$$
BalancedAccuracy\approx78.6\%.
$$

These results are **synthetic only**.

The important architectural conclusion is:

$$
\boxed{
ML can approximate the exact acquisition policy extremely well inside its learned regime while degrading materially outside it.
}
$$

Therefore the exact planner remains the reference oracle for validation.

# 46. Counterfactual validation of ML

We should not evaluate only:

$$
Accuracy(\hat\pi,\pi^*).
$$

We should also ask:

> What happens if the ML policy selects the wrong acquisition?

Therefore test:

$$
Regret(\hat\pi)
=
V^*(E)-V^{\hat\pi}(E).
$$

This is more meaningful than classification accuracy.

---

# 47. Acquisition regret

Define:

$$
\boxed{
Regret_\pi(E)
=
V^*(E)-V^\pi(E).
}
$$

Then an approximation contract may require:

$$
Regret_\pi(E)\leq\epsilon
$$

for a specified evaluation population and scope.

This is a far stronger ML assurance metric.

# 48. Why accuracy can be misleading

Suppose:

```text
STOP = 95% of cases
Acquire = 5%
```

An ML model predicting:

```text
STOP always
```

gets:

$$
Accuracy=95\%.
$$

But it may have catastrophic regret on the 5% cases where acquisition is essential.

Therefore:

$$
\boxed{
PolicyAccuracy\neq PolicyAdequacy.
}
$$

This fits the KnowledgeOS philosophy perfectly.

# 55. Policy tree

Mathematically:

$$
\pi(E)=a
$$

and after observation:

$$
\pi(E_{a,o})=a'.
$$

Thus a plan is a policy tree:

```text id="c3v6j5"
             A
          /     \
       o1         o2
       /           \
      B            STOP
     / \
   o3   o4
   │     │
 STOP  HumanReview
```

This is the correct computational representation of sequential acquisition.

# 57. Acquisition plan expiration
