## 0. In business language

The author takes the Relationship Ontology (S0021), **holds it fixed**, and asks whether it survives being applied to three products: PublicDigit (actual), a hospital information system and an ERP system (both hypothetical, "conceptual only"). The aim is not to prove the ontology correct but to see what breaks.

- **What held:** most concepts and most invariants. **Design policy** held best ("an order names a prescriber"; "an entry cites a source"). The restated I-11 (knowledge does not execute) held where the original wording would have failed.
- **What broke:**
  1. **I-4** (a projection is derived and cited as authority by nothing): a hospital discharge summary and a signed financial statement are generated **and** authoritative. A later correction withdraws the falsification: they became authoritative through attestation, so I-4 is incomplete, not false.
  2. **"One PKS per product"**: an ERP deployed to fifty customers with different charts of accounts. The model cannot tell a **product** from a **deployment** of it.
  3. **External authority**: regulators and accounting standards impose principles the organisation must obey but does not own. "The ontology can express who decides. It cannot express who must be obeyed."
- **The common shape of the gaps:** the four missing relations (specializes, constrains, instantiates, attests) all describe what happens **to** things, while the existing four (contains, references, generates, depends on) describe what things are made of. "The ontology describes composition. It does not describe transformation."
- **The methodological result:** PublicDigit **masked** two of the three failures. It has multi-tenancy in its code and treats election law as domain knowledge, so a single-product check could never have found them.

## 5. Arguments

- **Falsification by projection:** hold the model fixed, apply it to unlike domains, record what breaks (L5, L9–10). Survival is not proof; failure is information (L183).
- **Coherence of the gap:** four missing relations share one shape, so they are "far more likely to be a real omission than an artifact of my three examples" (L142).
- **Masking:** two failures exist in PublicDigit but are invisible in a model validated against PublicDigit alone; so cross-product projection is "justified by evidence, not by principle" (L167, L191).

1. **I-4 falsified (L91, L159, L188, L197) vs withdrawn (L73–79).** Two layers; the later block governs as the author's later statement.
2. **The tallies** (§7): L52 vs the §2 table vs §8 vs §5.

5. **I-10 vs F-3.** I-10 is said to hold "and strengthened" because "a regulation voids a conflicting internal policy" (L66). But F-3 says the ontology cannot express external authority at all (L108–117). The invariant is confirmed by exactly the kind of authority the model has no place for.
6. **Runtime adapter "disappears"** (L49, L149). [I] Hospital and ERP systems do have runtime integration layers; the concept "disappears" because the hypothetical PKSs were not given one. The disappearance may reflect how the examples were built, not the domains.
7. **"Transformational"** (L140): [I] the four missing relations are grouped by not being among the four structural verbs. "Specializes" is, in ordinary ontology practice, a structural (is-a) relation, and "constrains" (a regulation constrains a principle) is not something that happens to a thing over time. The "single coherent gap" may be partly a matter of definition. See §11.
8. **Hypothetical evidence:** the two new domains, and H-1's "strengthened" support (the ERP balance check, L150), are the author's constructions, not repository artifacts.

## 11. [I] Interpretation (kept apart)

- [I-4] **Ext (general knowledge, not looked up):** in ontology languages, specialization (sub-class, is-a) is the standard structural relation; the W3C RDF Schema's `rdfs:subClassOf` is the canonical example. This supports §10 item 7. It is background, not corpus evidence.
