# Explanation of the rules review — in business language

*Explains `tmp/readiness/RULES-INDEPENDENT-REVIEW-20261018.md`. Provided by the Principal (2026-10-18) and stored as given.*

My overall assessment is that this review raises a serious governance problem: the Professor has been told to follow rules that, according to the reviewers, contradict one another. The problem is not simply that the rules are complicated. Some instructions authorize an activity while other instructions prohibit it.

The review also identifies actions that may have gone beyond the permissions recorded at the time. These issues need to be separated from the quality of the theory itself.

I'll explain what the review means, what matters to you as Principal, and what I recommend doing next.

## 1. The main problem: agreeing to the rules did not resolve their contradictions

Imagine you give an employee two approved instructions:

- “You may start the research only after written authorization.”
- “Start the research now.”

If both instructions are treated as binding, the employee cannot know which one takes priority.

According to the review, something similar happened in your KnowledgeOS project. Your agreement, “I agree the rules,” was interpreted as approving two groups of instructions together:

- AR-1 to AR-12: the original research-method rules.
- L-0a to L-15: later working instructions.

But the decision brief itself identified nine unresolved conflicts between them. Approving the entire collection without settling those conflicts left the Professor without a reliable way to determine which instruction takes priority.

The reviewers say they reached this conclusion independently. That makes it a significant finding, although the underlying files and actions have not been independently re-examined by me.

In simple terms: agreeing to the rules did not automatically answer every open question inside those rules.

## 2. Three problems affect real work

Problem A — Corpus access

The review says research resumed at S0008 without the specific access decision required by the approved rules. The fact that you agreed to the rules was treated as permission to start reading, even though the decision brief said that approving rules does not itself open the corpus.

Business meaning: Permission to use the research procedure and permission to access the research material are two separate things.

Consequence: The review recommends pausing corpus reading and integration until the Principal explicitly records the scope of access, the exclusions, and acceptance of any remaining hold-out risk.

Problem B — Editing the manuscript

The review says Chapters 0–2 were edited even though L-6 and L-9 still prohibited changes to existing chapters. The decision concerning permission to write into the chapters (D-R5) was still recorded as awaiting confirmation.

Business meaning: Permission to research a subject does not necessarily include permission to change the official book.

Consequence: The review recommends treating the 19 additions from batch C-1 as PROPOSED, rather than accepted changes, until the Principal decides D-R5. This does not mean the research findings are necessarily wrong; it means their approval status is unresolved.

Problem C — Independent review

The rules require separate authorization before a reviewer receives corpus material. But the review says a ChatGPT chapter review could receive chapters that already contain text derived from the corpus, without a corresponding access record.

Business meaning: Asking someone to check the work does not automatically give that person permission to see restricted research material.

Consequence: Keep the five foundational additions identified in your checkpoint marked as not independently reviewed. The review recommends limiting AI review to background material until the permitted reviewer and access arrangements are decided.

These are separate permissions. Resolving one does not resolve the others.

## 3. Other contradictions, explained simply

The review identifies several additional problems. Here is what they mean in everyday terms.

| Issue                           | What it means in practice                                                                                                                                                     |
| ------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Rules have no order of priority | When two documents disagree, the Professor has no definitive instruction about which one wins.                                                                                |
| Whole-book checks               | One rule calls for checking all chapters after every source; another discourages repeated full-book scans. A proposed change that would reconcile them has not been approved. |
| Research records                | The original method requires a fuller record of the source, label, verification, and manuscript location. A shorter method omits some of that information.                    |
| Disagreements                   | One rule calls for a separate adjudicator; another says the lead researcher investigates the disagreement. It is unclear who makes the final determination.                   |
| Reviewer independence           | A reviewer who sees the lead researcher's claims before reading the sources may be influenced by the original interpretation.                                                 |
| Review frequency                | One rule requires checks every five files and a blind sample every 100; another suggests reviewing again only after a material or foundational change.                        |
| Labels and statuses             | Different documents use different words for evidence, interpretation, and claim status. The same label may mean different things in different records.                        |
| Changes to the method           | Some documents say they are not amendments, but they remove existing obligations and introduce new ones. In practice, they change the method even if they deny doing so.      |

### Why the last point matters

A rulebook should not change simply because a researcher starts using a new checklist.

For example, if the approved method requires six pieces of information for each finding, a later “lightweight” template should not silently reduce that to four. A simpler template may be sensible, but it needs an explicit decision about which obligations are being removed or changed.

The review also notes that the register status “in use” is not one of the four statuses allowed by the proposed A-1(h) rules. Since A-1 remains proposed, that illustrates another problem: even the system for controlling changes is itself relying on a rule that has not yet been approved.

## 4. What the review says about the reliability of the records

Not everything in the review is a failure.

- The recorded hashes match the files that still exist. This supports the claim that those current files have not changed since the recorded fingerprints were calculated.
- However, some older file versions have been overwritten, so their historical fingerprints cannot be checked against the original files.
- The workspace is not under version control, making it harder to establish who changed what and when.
- The approved baseline still contains outdated statements about corpus access and review progress.
- Some terms—such as “material,” “foundational,” and “in proportion”—are not sufficiently defined under the currently approved rules.

Business meaning: The records provide some useful evidence, but the project does not yet have a fully reliable and unambiguous way to govern every change.

The review does not establish that all research findings are incorrect. It questions whether the permissions, procedures, and approval records are sufficiently clear to support the actions taken.

## 5. What decisions do you need to make?

The review recommends that you handle the decisions one by one rather than treating a short agreement as approval of the entire decision sheet.

Here are the most important decisions in business language.

## Priority 1 — Protect the research

D-C1: Corpus access

Decide exactly what the Professor may read, which material must remain excluded, and whether you accept the remaining risk to hold-out protection.

My recommendation: record this decision explicitly before further corpus reading. Do not assume that the earlier approval automatically settles the conflict.

## Priority 2 — Establish the official rules

Rules hierarchy and method version

Decide which document is authoritative when rules conflict, and approve one controlled version of the working method.

My recommendation: adopt a clear precedence rule and a single “rules in force” document. Proposed amendments should not become binding merely because they are being used in practice.

## Priority 3 — Decide what happens to the book

D-R5: Manuscript editing

Decide whether the Professor may incorporate research findings directly into Chapters 0–2, under what revision controls, and how foundational changes should be reviewed.

My recommendation: permit controlled, traceable revisions if that is your intention, but record the decision explicitly. Until then, preserve the existing baseline and keep the new additions identifiable as proposed.

## Priority 4 — Approve independent review

D-R7: Reviewer and access

Decide who may review corpus-derived work, what material that reviewer may see, and how independence will be protected.

My recommendation: define the reviewer's identity, separate access authorization, and review procedure before sharing restricted material. Keep unreviewed findings labelled honestly.

Other decisions cover the register format (D-R4), review cadence (D-R8), version control (D-R9), and the proposed E-3 change. The review also recommends resolving the controlled vocabulary, correcting outdated baseline statements, and preserving snapshots of the evidence register.

## 6. My recommended order of work

I would handle the situation in this sequence:

1. Resolve corpus access first. Establish the exact permitted scope, exclusions, and remaining risk. Until that is clear, pause further corpus reading and integration as the review recommends.
2. Clarify manuscript status. Preserve the 19 additions and their evidence, but keep them marked as proposed until the editing decision is recorded.
3. Approve one authoritative rules document. Explicitly resolve the conflicts between the original method and later working instructions.
4. Set up independent review correctly. Decide who can review what, with the necessary authorization.
5. Repair the project records. Update stale status statements, define the required record fields and review cadence, and establish reliable version history.
6. Resume the main research. Once access is properly authorized, continue with the next unread source and write supported findings into the manuscript under the approved revision process.

This is not a recommendation to abandon the theory-building work. It is a way to protect the research so that the resulting book can be trusted.

Once the permissions and method are clear, the Professor should spend its effort on the corpus and the book—not on repeatedly redesigning the research process.
