## 1.12 A record of an act is not the act

*Sources:* DR-AB, the ARB's decision record, and SI, the script capability evidence inventory (paths under References; their descriptions are in the chapter's source list, `01.00-index.md`). Two later corpus sources, S0095 and S0090, are cited as further evidence where they bear on a claim. *Status:* the claims keep the status they had; this section's revision history is in `01.99-change-log.md`.

### 1.12.0 In plain language

A record of an act is not the act (1.12-1). A machine can check only records, so a history of standing can be shown *verifiable*, that is, every act has a record of sufficient integrity, but not *valid*, that is, the acts occurred. The second rests on trust in the recording process: the residual trust assumption of the authority model (1.12-2). The same holds for every self-report: records, reports of enforcement and self-descriptions are claims about events, to be evidenced (1.12-3). The platform's own enforcement shows three silent fail-opens, each a declaration that diverged from behaviour. Its cure, refusing to run when the policy cannot be read, is applied only to some gates, and catches unreadable values, not missing ones (1.12-4).

### 1.12.1 Claims and their arguments

**1.12-1. A report of an act is not the act (Src, DR-AB L9).**
DR-AB records the ARB's decisions and states its own limit: *"A report of an act is not the act: if a separate ratification ceremony is required, this record is its input, not its substitute."* Where a separate ratification is required, the record is offered as input to that act, not as a replacement for it.

**1.12-2. A record does not make the act happen: verifiable is not valid (Ded, from §1.2 and 1.12-1).**
§1.2 says that every change of standing requires a *recorded* act. 1.12-1 adds the converse caution: **a record does not make the act happen.** A machine can check only records, never acts. Two properties of a standing history therefore come apart:
- it is *valid* if the acts occurred, which is a fact about the world;
- it is *verifiable* if every act has a record of sufficient integrity: who, when, under which grant, with what scope.

Verification can establish only the second. The first rests on trust in the recording process, for example that the record was written by the deciding person and not by an assistant on their behalf. This is the **residual trust assumption** of the whole authority model, and the theory states it rather than hiding it.

*Further evidence (supporting; the status of the claim is unchanged).* A later disposition in the corpus shows the assumption at work. A missing START could have been transcribed into the record, but "A transcription records **a human act that was given**. No such act exists in this lane's record, and **Governance may not supply one.**" [S0095 L141]. The occurrence of the act had to come from outside the record, from the person entitled to state it. And the case the claim names, a record written by someone other than the decider, occurs in the corpus: a verifier "**transcribed the PO/ARB's `START` act into the workflow record myself**" (S0090 L30, in meaning), and the same line states that "**recording an act is not granting one**" (S0090 L30, in meaning). *What this evidence does not show:* how the residual trust assumption can be discharged. Both cases leave it to an act outside the record. *A related but distinct distinction,* between whether a work product's claims hold and who is answerable for them, is in §8.9 C1.

**1.12-3. Every self-report is a claim about an event, to be evidenced (Ded; with Ch. 2 §2.8.7).**
The argument of 1.12-2 does not depend on the record being a record of an *act*. It holds for any **self-report**. A system's report of its own environment is a claim with provenance *self*, not a fact: the framework reports "production" on a developer's machine. A check's message "BLOCKED" was not a block when the exit code did not block. Records of acts, reports of enforcement and self-descriptions are all **claims about events, to be evidenced**, never the events themselves. This connects to §1.2's *authored-by* requirement: the record must name its producer. The platform's own evidence for this claim is 1.12-4.

**1.12-4. The platform's enforcement failed silently: declaration diverged from behaviour (Src, observed; SI L117–126, L160–167, L263–273, L334–341, L356, L392).**
The inventory records three incidents in which the **enforcement layer failed silently**, each time with a reassuring visible symptom:
1. a guard printed "BLOCKED" but exited with a code that did not block, so "the destructive command executed regardless";
2. a counterfeit tool answered every query with empty output, so the gates "run on empty configuration and **fail open**: they pass without measuring anything";
3. a guard's path could not be resolved, so it "did not run at all … an absent gate reports nothing and blocks nothing".

To these come a component whose header describes a file path its code does not write, and a registry entry "planned" for five weeks for a mechanism that does not exist: "the registry describing an intention, not an inventory" [SI L392].

*Reading (Ded):* in each case a **declaration diverged from behaviour**: a printed message, a declared hook wiring, a header, or a registry entry. Incident 3 produced no report at all; there the divergence was between the wiring declaration and what ran. These are the self-reports of 1.12-3, found in the platform's own enforcement.

*The platform's cure, and its limit.* The platform states a **meta-rule**: "a governance gate must **refuse to run** when it cannot read its policy" (**verified**, `scripts/lib/config-guard.sh` L6). In the terms of Ch. 2 §2.8.8, a gate whose policy is unreadable is in E6(c) and must fail closed. The inventory notes that the cure is applied only to some gates [SI L273]. And it has a further limit (**verified**, `scripts/design-check.sh` L42–46): the design gate fills **missing** settings with hard-coded defaults (for example threshold 150, `exit_on_fail` false). An absent policy value is therefore silently replaced by a specified one, the failure Ch. 2 §2.8.8 names ("E2 silently replaced by a specified value"). The guard catches unreadable values, not missing ones.

### 1.12.98 Open questions

- No question is stated as open in this section. Two limits are stated: the residual trust assumption, which the theory states rather than hides (1.12-2), and the reach of the platform's cure, applied only to some gates and blind to missing values (1.12-4).

### 1.12.99 What does not hold

- **"Every one is a self-report diverging"** (an earlier reading of the evidence of 1.12-4). In incident 3 the guard produced no report at all; the divergence there is between the wiring declaration and what ran (the reading now in 1.12-4).
