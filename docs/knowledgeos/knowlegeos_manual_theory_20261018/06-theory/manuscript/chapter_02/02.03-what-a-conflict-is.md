## 2.3 What a conflict is

**The idea.** Two rules conflict iff there is a possible state of the world where **both apply and their effects cannot both be satisfied** [RM L149]. Worked example [RM L154–162]:
- R1: production APIs must use OAuth2;
- R2: internal production APIs must not.

The world "production and internal" makes both apply, and no assignment satisfies both. **Src**, adopted by RM as *the* definition of conflict.

RM credits the definition with two properties: it is *decidable* and it is *explanatory*. Its output is the **region** where the contradiction holds, not a yes/no [RM L165–168].

**Assessment (Ded): correct, with three refinements.**

**(a) "Decidable" holds only for a restricted language.** Whether ∃(w, t) with both rules applying and their effects jointly unsatisfiable is a satisfiability question. It is decidable when applicability and effects are written in a decidable logic, e.g.:
- propositional formulas over finitely many attributes;
- linear arithmetic over thresholds.

It is **not decidable in general**. Precisely (refined after audit L-10): first-order logic over a *fixed finite* attribute domain is decidable. Satisfiability is decidable for, e.g., propositional logic, linear arithmetic and real closed fields; it is undecidable for, e.g., non-linear integer arithmetic. The theory must therefore *restrict* the applicability language, or accept that some comparisons end in UNKNOWN. RM's UNKNOWN value [RM L191] is not an optional nicety. It is **logically necessary** whenever the language is expressive or the solver is bounded (even decidable fragments can time out).

*Note on RM's word:* RM's "decidable" [RM L167] means **determinate**, "the answer does not depend on who asks", not algorithmically decidable. My point (a) addresses a different notion. Both hold: a conflict, when found, is determinate; finding it may not be computable.

**(b) "Cannot both be satisfied" covers only part of deontic conflict.** In standard deontic logic:
- MUST φ = O(φ);
- MUST_NOT φ = O(¬φ);
- MAY φ = P(φ) = ¬O(¬φ).

Then:

| Pair | Conflict? | Why |
|---|---|---|
| MUST φ vs MUST_NOT φ | **yes** | O(φ) ∧ O(¬φ) has no compliant state. This is RM's case |
| **MAY φ vs MUST_NOT φ** | **yes, but not by "satisfaction"** | P(φ) ∧ O(¬φ) is *logically inconsistent*: one rule permits what the other forbids. Yet no *action* violates both, because a permission is not "satisfied" by acting. RM's definition **misses this class** |
| SHOULD φ vs MUST_NOT φ | **not a logical conflict** | SHOULD is defeasible. How strong effects dominate weak ones is a **governance policy, not logic**, as RM itself says [RM L137, OQ-13] |
| MUST φ vs MUST φ′ | conflict only if φ ∧ φ′ is unsatisfiable on the intersection | e.g. "use TLS 1.3" vs "use TLS 1.2 only" |

**Refined definition (Ded):**

> r₁ and r₂ **conflict** iff there exist (w, t) where both apply and **either** their hard effects (MUST, MUST_NOT) cannot be jointly satisfied, **or** one permits (MAY) what the other forbids (MUST_NOT) or demands the negation of (MUST ¬φ).

> **Revised after RS (§2.0, §2.11).** Under RM-1 a *rule* is an obligation. The deontic role of **permission against a rule** belongs to the **exception**, a separately authorized deviation (RM-4). So the "MAY vs MUST_NOT" case is one of two things:
> - an **exception**, if the permitting statement is a recorded, authorized exception whose scope lies within the rule's scope and whose validity is current;
> - otherwise an **unauthorised permission that contradicts a rule**, which *is* a conflict to report.
>
> This turns my deontic refinement into a precise test: **a permission overlapping a prohibition is legitimate iff it is an authorized exception.** *Classification of the change: REVISION of my own earlier text.*
>
> **Built into the test (audit L-07).** In standard deontic logic, P(φ) ∧ O(¬φ) is inconsistent whether or not P comes from an authorized exception. The "legitimate" reading needs the **defeasible** model, in which exceptions **narrow the rule**. The conflict test is run on the rule's **effective applicability**:
>
> A′ᵣ(w, t) = Aᵣ(w) ∧ w ∈ Sᵣ ∧ t ∈ Tᵣ ∧ ¬(E₁(w, t) ∨ … ∨ Eₖ(w, t))
>
> Here E₁…Eₖ are the regions of the rule's **current, authorized, scope-contained** exceptions. Two consequences:
> - a valid exception removes its region from the rule, so no conflict arises there;
> - a permission that is *not* such an exception does not narrow anything and is reported as its own finding class, **"unauthorised permission against a rule"**, separate from rule-vs-rule conflicts.
>
> **Final definition (supersedes the refined definition above):** r₁ and r₂ **conflict** iff there exist (w, t) in A′₁ ∩ A′₂ where their **mandatory** effects cannot be jointly satisfied.
>
> **Corrected by (d) below:** this definition is pairwise, and it lets (w, t) both fix the situation and be what must "satisfy" the effects. The set-wise definition (d) supersedes it; a pair is its two-rule case.

> Pairs involving SHOULD / SHOULD_NOT are **tensions**, resolved by a stated dominance policy, never reported as proven conflicts.

**(c) The region is the result.** I agree with RM: the useful output is the set {(w, t) : both apply ∧ the effects clash}, the "applicable intersection" [RM L225]. A conflict therefore has a **place and a time span**. Two rules can conflict only during the overlap of their validity intervals [RM L245–248].


**(d) Conflict is a property of rule sets, with context and choice kept apart (Ded).**
- **Context and choice.** In the final definition, (w, t) both fixes the situation and is what has to "satisfy" the effects. If w fixes everything, effects are simply true or false in w, and "cannot be satisfied" has no content. Split w into the **context** c, what the actor does not control (the API is production and internal), and the **outcome** x, what the actor controls (the authentication it uses). Applicability depends on c and t only, never on x (a scope assumption: a rule whose applicability depends on what the actor does is outside (d)); effects φᵢ(c, x) constrain x.
- **Definition.** A finite set ℛ = {r₁, …, rₙ} of rules is **in conflict** iff there are (c, t) with (c, t) ∈ A′₁ ∩ … ∩ A′ₙ (all apply, on their effective applicability) and no x with φ₁(c, x) ∧ … ∧ φₙ(c, x) (their mandatory effects). Two rules conflict iff the pair is in conflict: the pairwise definition is the case n = 2.
- **What is reported: minimal conflicting sets, point by point.** At a point (c, t), let ℛ(c, t) be the rules that apply there. A set S ⊆ ℛ(c, t) is **minimal in conflict at (c, t)** iff no x satisfies all of S's effects at (c, t), and every proper subset of S is satisfiable there. The report lists each such S with its **region**: the points where S is minimal in conflict. A conflicting pair is the case |S| = 2.
- **Why point by point (a counterexample to the global form).** Defined globally ("a set in conflict none of whose proper subsets is in conflict anywhere"), minimality loses whole regions when effects depend on the context. R1: MUST (p ∨ q) and R3: MUST ¬q apply everywhere; R2 is MUST ¬p at c1 and MUST (¬p ∧ ¬q) at c2. Then {R1, R2} is in conflict at c2 only, and {R1, R2, R3} at c1 and c2. Globally, {R1, R2} is the only minimal set, and the conflict at c1 is reported nowhere; point by point, {R1, R2, R3} is minimal at c1 (checked: `tmp/tools/conflict_minimality_check.py`). If no effect depends on the context, the two forms report the same regions (Ded).
- **Pairwise checking is not enough (counterexample).** R1: MUST (p ∨ q); R2: MUST ¬p; R3: MUST ¬q, all applying everywhere. Each pair is satisfiable: {R1, R2} by q, {R1, R3} by p, {R2, R3} by ¬p ∧ ¬q. The three together are not. A pairwise test reports no conflict; the set-wise test reports {R1, R2, R3} as a minimal conflicting set. **This example is the flagged case.**
- **When pairs do suffice (theory turn 5, §9.14; provisional, CON-0192).** Pairs (and single rules), tested point by point, give exactly the set-wise report for **every** base over an admitted rule language **iff** the language's effect class has Helly number at most 2 (the converse under realisability). For a single base, pairs suffice iff its largest minimal conflicting set has at most two rules.
  - **On a full product of independent attributes**, h = 2 holds for: required values with thresholds on one attribute; required and forbidden values on a Boolean or infinite (declared open) attribute; Boolean literals; products of these.
  - **It fails for:** thresholds together with forbidden interior values on one attribute; forbidden values or allowed sets on a finite attribute with three or more values; some partial orders (not product orders or trees) and cyclic orders with arcs of every length (§9.14 v4); cardinality over members; linear inequalities across attributes; any coupling between attributes; and disjunctions such as R1 here.
  - The counterexample is a disjunction, so it is outside the pairs-suffice class.
- **Monotone on the common region:** at a point (c, t) where every rule of ℛ′ ⊇ ℛ applies, if ℛ is in conflict there, so is ℛ′. Outside the common region a superset may not conflict, because not all of its rules apply.
- **Cost.** There are up to 2ⁿ subsets, so finding every minimal conflicting set can be exponential in n. "S is in conflict" has the form ∃(c, t) ∀x ¬(φ₁ ∧ … ∧ φₙ): one quantifier alternation. §2.9's gate question, "for every (c, t) in the gated region, some x satisfies all applicable effects", is its negation, ∀∃, with the same alternation. Both are decidable in the fragments named in (a) (for example, by quantifier elimination in linear arithmetic); in the propositional case they lie at the second level of the polynomial-time hierarchy, which is defined by such alternations (*Ext:* Stockmeyer 1976), and are in general not plain satisfiability checks. §2.6 bounds n by partitioning.
- **Prior work (Ext).** That constraints satisfiable two at a time can be jointly unsatisfiable is the established gap between local consistency (arc and path consistency) and global consistency in constraint networks (Mackworth 1977; Freuder 1978, 1982). Minimal unsatisfiable subsets are an established object with enumeration algorithms (Liffiton and Sakallah 2008). (d) **adapts** them: what is new here is only the split of context from outcome and the point-by-point region, which is what a conflict report for rules needs. Not shown new: the search covered these works only.
- **Not covered:** permissions against rules (b) and SHOULD tensions keep their own finding classes; (d) concerns mandatory effects only.

**(e) Liveness, beside safety (Ded).** The conditions above, and those of §2.6 and §2.8.8, say what an analyser must **not** report: no false "consistent", no missed overlap. An analyser that answers UNKNOWN for everything satisfies all of them, and so does a partition with one block. So a usefulness requirement is needed beside them: **every case that is decidable in the restricted language (a) must be decided** (CONFLICTS, with its region, or CONSISTENT). UNKNOWN is then reserved for the undecidable, unresolved or time-out cases of E6 (§2.8.8). How much decided output is enough is a separate, stated requirement, not part of soundness.
