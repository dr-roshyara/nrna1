## Part A — The Question

OQ-38B05-05 asked: should the three trust roots — **Legitimacy** (who defines rules), **Authenticity** (who verifies evidence), and **Temporal** (who records phases) — be **structurally separated** into independent constitutional sources, or may they **share a single sovereign source** protected by strong constitutional safeguards?

---

## Part B — The Evidence

### B.1 — Internal discovery (Rounds 38A–38C)
Internal analysis identified a structural vulnerability: when all three trust roots trace to one sovereign source, the architecture lacks an independent vantage point from which to evaluate the constitutional legitimacy of that sovereign's exercise of its trust-function grants (Constitutional External Reference Deficit). It further identified that the body interpreting constitutional independence is appointed by the same sovereign whose independence is interpreted — a self-reference manageable in mature systems but more dangerous in young ones (Meta-CVI).

### B.2 — Comparative constitutional evidence
Across eight mechanisms (Germany's eternity clause, India's basic-structure doctrine, separation-of-powers, constitutional-court models, and others), the evidence unanimously supports **functional independence** as the constitutional norm. No studied mechanism requires structural independence as a baseline; constitutional courts, election commissions, and oversight bodies trace authority to the order they check, protected by strong operational safeguards.

### B.3 — Constitutional infancy finding
Comparative evidence corroborates that constitutional infancy — absence of institutional history, judicial culture, independent press, rival parties, established norms — affects the reliability of governance mechanisms. The same text may be safe in a mature system and risky in a young one. This does **not** make functional independence impossible; it makes additional protections **necessary**.

### B.4 — Comparative / model-assisted corroboration (Stream 2; not human validation)
Independent comparative and model-assisted analyses, working from the *neutral* problem statement without the program's internal vocabulary, recognised the same tensions the program discovered internally: parliamentary-omnipotence risk, self-reference vulnerability, constitutional-infancy concern, and lawful gradual erosion (the democratic-backsliding / abusive-constitutionalism pattern). **This corroborates the problem formulation** — it confirms the concern is recognised in constitutional theory and is not an artifact of internal vocabulary. **It does not constitute independent human-expert validation** (correlated sources; human n = 0). It validates the *problem*, not the *choice*.

## Part C — The Ruling

### C.1 — Model selection
**Option B — Functional Independence — is SELECTED.** The three trust roots shall continue to trace constitutional authority to a single sovereign source. Structural separation into independent constitutional sources is **not required**.

### C.2 — Rationale
Functional independence is the constitutional norm across all mature democratic systems studied. It preserves unified constitutional authority, avoids the complexity of multiple sovereign sources, and is consistent with keeping all governance internal.

Structural independence would require constitutionally distinct trust authorities with independent legitimacy protections. **The ARB finds that such separation is not necessary for NRNA's constitutional objectives** — not that it is incoherent. The risks of a single-source model are real but, in the ARB's judgment, manageable through permanent constitutional safeguards.

### C.3 — Constitutional infancy acknowledged
The organisation is in constitutional infancy and lacks the surrounding institutional ecology that makes functional independence durable in mature systems. This is a **risk factor**. It does **not** mandate structural separation; it mandates **stronger** constitutional protections than a mature system would require. NRNA substitutes permanent safeguards for the institutional maturity it does not yet possess.

### C.4 — Permanent safeguards
Adopted as **permanent** features of the constitutional order — not transitional, not sunsetting, not contingent on maturity milestones:

**S-1 — Amendment Protection.** Provisions establishing oversight-body independence, jurisdictional scope, and interpretive finality shall be protected at a constitutional amendment threshold **exceeding all ordinary constitutional amendment procedures**. *(The precise threshold — e.g., near-unanimity, multi-cycle or regional ratification — remains subject to Governance Capability Discovery; S-1 fixes the requirement, not yet the number.)*

**S-2 — Appointment Diversity.** No single Assembly cohort or election cycle shall appoint a majority of any constitutional oversight body. Terms shall be staggered, non-renewable, and longer than any single Assembly term. Removal shall require supermajority authorization on defined constitutional grounds only.

**S-3 — Jurisdictional Protection.** The mandate of constitutional oversight bodies shall explicitly include election oversight and trust-function evaluation, and shall not be narrowed or stripped by ordinary amendment.

**S-4 — Challenge Pathways.** When the independence of an oversight body is itself challenged, adjudication shall route through a **distinct constitutional review mechanism whose members are not participants in the challenged proceeding** (recusal of the challenged body; standing for constitutional actors outside it to initiate the challenge). *This is a functional separation of role within the single constitutional source — it does not create a separate sovereign authority.*

**S-5 — Finality with Defined Exceptions.** Rulings within jurisdiction shall be constitutionally final; override shall require amendment at the S-1 threshold, not a simple majority. Retroactive invalidation upon discovery of fundamental defects (OQ-38A05-02) remains reserved for the Constitutional Interpretation Chamber.

**Recorded residual risk.** S-4 provides the maximal *functional* answer to "who checks the checker," but within a single constitutional source there is **no structurally external vantage point.** Meta-CVI is therefore **managed, not eliminated.** Option B is adopted in explicit, recorded acceptance of this residual — which is the standing reason the Part E reservation on Option A is retained.

> **The safeguards adopted in this ruling reduce, but do not eliminate, the residual risks identified during the 38C analysis series (CERD, Meta-CVI, progressive lawful erosion). Functional Independence remains a managed-risk model, not a risk-free model.**

## Part D — Constitutional Maturity

Constitutional maturity — that the reliability of governance mechanisms depends on surrounding institutional ecology — is accepted as an **architectural finding**. It explains why the same design may suit different systems with different safeguards. It does **not** create a constitutional doctrine, maturity trigger, certification process, or transition authority. The Part C.4 safeguards are permanent, not maturity-gated.

---

## Part E — Deselections

**Option A — Structural Independence — is DESELECTED.** The ARB judges structural separation not necessary for NRNA's objectives. **Option A is deselected, not refuted; it remains constitutionally coherent and available** should the permanent safeguards prove inadequate in operation (reservation retained).

**Option C — Phased Model — is DESELECTED.** Permanent safeguards are chosen over a maturity-gated transition, avoiding a secondary governance problem around maturity definition, measurement, certification, and challenge.
